Put the outputs on the same page

The IEA's April 2026 report, Annex A, Tables A.3 and A.4, reports 183 TWh of U.S. data-center electricity in 2024 and 426 TWh in its 2030 base case. The 2030 total contains 345 TWh for IT equipment; the remainder is facility overhead. Its published U.S. aggregate power usage effectiveness (PUE) is 1.23, rounded. Berkeley Lab's June 2026 update estimates 192 TWh in 2024 and 649 TWh in its 2030 reference case, with a reported 2030 aggregate PUE of 1.36. Both organizations build demand from installed equipment and facility energy. The IEA explicitly credits Berkeley Lab's bottom-up modeling lineage, but uses its own shipment, allocation, utilization and facility assumptions.

Their 2024 estimates differ by 9 TWh. Their 2030 estimates differ by 223 TWh. Thus 214 TWh of the later gap comes from different projected growth from their respective 2024 estimates, an arithmetic observation rather than an explanation of why their growth assumptions differ. Averaging 426 and 649 TWh would erase that disagreement without resolving it.

What can be reconciled from published numbers

For each model, total facility electricity approximately equals IT electricity times aggregate PUE. The IEA's published rounded 2030 totals imply an effective ratio of 426 / 345 = 1.2348, consistent with the published 1.23 after rounding. Berkeley Lab's 649 TWh total and 1.36 PUE imply about 649 / 1.36 = 477.2 TWh of IT electricity. This second number is derived from rounded values, not an independently reported IT total. It suggests a gap of roughly 132 TWh in IT energy before facility overhead. The two models' published aggregates are not detailed enough to determine how much of that difference comes from shipment counts, equipment power, operating hours, lifetimes, geographic allocation or facility mix.

A sequential bridge makes the remaining arithmetic visible:

Reversing the order gives about 163.2 TWh for the IT step and 59.8 TWh for the PUE step. The ordering changes the split because energy is the product of IT load and PUE. Consequently, the honest published-data statement is that an illustrative accounting bridge allocates about 43–60 TWh to the aggregate-PUE step and 163–180 TWh to the IT-energy step, depending on order and rounding. These are not causal attributions. Equipment mix can itself change aggregate PUE, and each model's published PUE is a weighted result of its facility mix.

An important boundary remains: the IEA's 100 GW of 2030 U.S. installed facility capacity in Table A.2 reflects installed equipment, not unused rack design capacity. Berkeley Lab's 148 GW interconnection requirement is calculated from 649 TWh using an assumed 50% utilization of connection capacity, a quantity it says is poorly documented. Those GW figures have different denominators and cannot be subtracted to measure a capacity disagreement. Energy is the comparable endpoint here.

What the sources can and cannot resolve

The reports provide clues, not a full parameter bridge. The IEA says its server shipment projections start with IDC 2024 data through 2028, triangulated with other research, and allocates global IT capacity to regions using commercial market data and other indicators. Berkeley Lab's update uses IDC 2025 Q3 accelerator data through 2030, Omdia inputs, revised AI server configuration and power assumptions, and a 2030 shipment projection of 19 million accelerators. Berkeley Lab says 2024 historical estimates were revised partly because reported 2023–24 GPU shipments changed. These are distinct information sets and model implementations; neither public report publishes a matched U.S. cohort table that allows a clean subtraction by parameter.

Berkeley Lab's own one-at-a-time scenarios test the importance of some assumptions: 578 TWh for lower accelerator deployment, 590 for shorter lifetime, 664 for higher ASIC shipments and 782 for higher inference energy, against 649 reference. They are within-model sensitivities, so subtracting one from 649 does not identify the corresponding difference from the IEA. Its compounded 521–843 TWh span is a stress range, not a probability interval. The IEA's 426 TWh lies below even that range, which is evidence that the two models' central inputs or structure differ beyond the tested Berkeley Lab perturbations. It is not evidence that one organization made an error.

The next defensible step is a joint cohort worksheet: matched U.S. geography and facility definition; annual conventional-server, GPU and ASIC shipments; accelerator units per server; survival curves; rated and idle power; training and inference utilization; storage and networking loads; facility mix and PUE; and any assumption about grid or chip-supply constraints. The worksheet would recompute 2024 and 2030 energy for each model with one variable family changed at a time, then document order dependence or use a symmetric decomposition. Proprietary IDC and Omdia inputs may prevent public replication. Until those inputs are available, the bridge here stops at the IT-energy versus aggregate-PUE accounting split, and the article should keep both forecasts visible.
