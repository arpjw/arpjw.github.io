An electric grid can look healthy on a Tuesday and still be in trouble for the next decade. On that Tuesday, operators match generation to consumption, keep frequency close to 60 hertz, and restore ordinary faults. In the planning office, however, a proposed data center asks for a connection larger than a city's existing demand. A utility orders transformers it may wait years to receive. A transmission line needs permission from several jurisdictions. A plant scheduled to retire is still counted on during a winter peak. None of these facts implies that the lights will go out tomorrow. Together they ask whether the United States can build physical capacity at the pace of its technological ambitions.

The temptation is to choose one number and declare a crisis. North American planners now project 224 gigawatts of summer peak-demand growth over a decade, up from 132 GW in the prior assessment. That is a forecast of a momentary maximum, covering Canada and parts of Mexico as well as the United States. It is not 224 GW of plants already needed, nor 224 GW of constant consumption. NERC, 2025 Long-Term Reliability Assessment1 Berkeley Lab's reference scenario puts U.S. data centers at 649 terawatt-hours in 2030, or 11.8% of modeled U.S. electricity consumption, with a broad 521–843 TWh uncertainty range. That is annual energy, produced by a model of equipment shipments and use, not a census of meters. LBNL, 2025 update2

The distinction between these numbers is the beginning of the story. Electricity must be sufficient in total, available at the dangerous hour, and deliverable at the exact point of use. It must also be affordable and secure. A surplus of solar nameplate capacity in one state does not energize a transformer in another. A data-center reservation is not an operating server. A successful attack on one distribution substation is not a national grid collapse. Treating these as separate questions makes the risks more concrete and the solutions less theatrical.

I. The machine that powers America#

1. An hour that must balance#

Electricity is produced and consumed almost simultaneously. Operators schedule generators and imports ahead of time, then continually adjust output, storage, and demand to respond to deviations. Frequency gives a system-wide clue about imbalance, though local voltage and network constraints matter as well. Protection equipment isolates faults so an event does not propagate. The network's apparent smoothness is therefore the result of active control, reserves, and contingency planning, not a passive flow from plant to outlet.

The United States has three major synchronized interconnections: Eastern, Western, and most of Texas. Their limited ties make a national total a poor guide to local adequacy. Even within an interconnection, a constrained line can separate a cheap supply region from an expensive load pocket. The relevant question for an industrial project is not whether the country can generate another gigawatt somewhere. It is whether the local network can provide the required power under credible outages and during the hours when other customers also need it. The DOE's draft transmission needs study3 catalogues precisely this geography of congestion and transfer limitations; it does not prescribe a single nationwide build list.

For a data center, this physics is more than background. Most of the electrical system still sees its campus as a load, regardless of whether GPUs are training a model or answering an ordinary request. The operator must know what the facility might draw at its connection, how quickly that draw can change, and what its controls do during a voltage disturbance. If thousands of power supplies disconnect together after a fault, the resulting loss of load can be as consequential to frequency control as a sudden loss of generation, though in the opposite direction. The safe response is careful modeling and commissioning, not an assumption that every new server behaves like the historical average commercial customer. NERC's computational-load alert4 made exactly these operational tasks explicit in 2026.

It also helps to distinguish reliability from resilience. Reliability asks whether service is maintained under defined conditions; resilience asks how a system absorbs, adapts to, and recovers from disruptive events. A plant can satisfy a capacity requirement yet share a floodplain, gas pipeline, or transformer supplier with its nominal backup. The loss of both during one event defeats a spreadsheet that treats them as independent. Conversely, a well-built network can survive an unexpected outage even if the component that failed was thought to be indispensable. Good system design is therefore less about eliminating every failure than about avoiding common-mode failures, detecting trouble early, and restoring service when prevention fails.

2. Five quantities that should never be swapped#

The machine is synchronized, but the risks are not. A sudden generator trip is an operational problem measured in seconds. A hot evening with too few dependable resources is an adequacy problem measured in hours. A transformer factory that cannot deliver a unit until 2029 is a planning problem measured in years. An outage at a distribution feeder may be severe for the people it serves while leaving the bulk system stable. FERC's reliability explainer5 separates reliable operation from resource adequacy for this reason. The first concerns the system's response to disturbances; the second concerns whether it has sufficient resources to meet demand. An honest risk assessment must say which one it means.

Consider what happens in the first seconds after a large plant trips. The electrical and mechanical energy stored in spinning machines initially absorbs part of the imbalance. Automatic controls and fast-responding resources then act, while operators can redispatch and call reserves. Modern inverter-based resources can provide some of these services too, subject to their controls, headroom, and grid-forming capabilities. The fact that power electronics can act quickly does not remove the need to model their behavior under faults or to coordinate protection settings. FERC's description of ancillary services6 includes regulation to hold frequency near 60 cycles per second and black-start capability to restore an area after a major interruption. Neither service is equivalent to buying enough annual energy.

The phrase 'the grid' also conceals multiple layers of control. A balancing authority keeps supply and demand aligned in its area. A transmission operator manages flows and contingencies on high-voltage facilities. Utilities and distribution operators attend to the lower-voltage network. A regional market can dispatch generation economically without owning every line or power plant. Responsibility is divided because the system spans private, municipal, cooperative, state, and federal institutions. Those boundaries often matter more to the speed of construction than a national engineering diagram suggests.

Planners commonly design for a defined set of contingencies. A single major element can fail without the network losing stability or shedding firm load, provided flows, voltage, and reserves stay within limits. But planning criteria are conditional. A wildfire may take several nearby lines out together. A freeze may disable many gas units or interrupt fuel upstream. A cyber compromise could degrade visibility just as equipment fails. The question is not whether the system passes one standard test; it is how its margins change when hazards are correlated. The 2026 FERC–NERC winter review7 is a good example of why real events matter: advance preparation and coordination kept service intact even while gas and power systems were strained.

Energy is accumulated electricity use, measured in kilowatt-hours or terawatt-hours. Power is a rate, measured in kilowatts or gigawatts. A 1 GW facility drawing at full power for a year uses 8.76 TWh; at a constant 90% load factor it uses 7.884 TWh. Nameplate capacity is a machine's rated output. Accredited or dependable capacity is the portion a planner expects to be available when reliability is at risk. Deliverable capacity includes what the wires and substations can carry to the customer. A battery also has a duration: 1 GW for four hours is 4 GWh, not 1 GW of supply through a three-day storm.

These distinctions explain why seemingly contradictory headlines can both be true. The EIA expected8 nearly 4,390 TWh of U.S. electric-power-sector generation in 2026, a record. PJM nevertheless reported9 that its auction for the 2028–29 delivery year cleared 6.831 GW less dependable capacity than its planning requirement. The first describes projected nationwide energy across a year. The second describes a regional future capacity procurement against a reliability target. Neither says how often a household lost power in 2026.

There is a subtler issue in the word 'peak.' A load's own maximum is not necessarily its contribution to the system maximum. If an industrial plant draws 1 GW at 2 a.m. and 200 MW on a hot afternoon, a system with a late-afternoon risk hour does not need to reserve 1 GW of capacity for that plant at that hour. If its load is flat through the day, the full 1 GW might be relevant. The calculation changes again if the local substation is constrained by its own maximum, which can occur at a different hour from the regional system peak. A planner therefore needs at least two time series: the customer's load and the rest of the system's load. Adding maxima from different hours manufactures an impossible peak.

The planning margin itself is a choice about risk. PJM's 2028–29 auction procured 149.182 GW of accredited capacity including its Fixed Resource Requirement resources, 6.831 GW below a 156.013 GW reliability requirement. PJM's auction report10 states plainly that this does not necessarily mean PJM cannot serve load in that delivery year; it indicates slimmer reserves and greater risk. The report says the auction cleared at a 14.4% installed reserve margin, below the 20% level corresponding to its one-day-in-ten-years loss-of-load expectation criterion. That criterion is a probabilistic planning standard, not a promise that a blackout will occur exactly once per decade. A change in weather, plant performance, or the load forecast changes the modeled risk.

Energy arithmetic can be useful when kept in its lane. Berkeley Lab's 649 TWh data-center reference case converts to about 74 GW of average power. If the same fleet used half of its aggregate connection rating on average, it would imply about 148 GW of grid connection. Change the assumed ratio to 70%, and the implied rating falls near 106 GW. The annual energy estimate has not changed at all. A chart showing one of these numbers without its assumption can create an apparent infrastructure crisis or apparent relief with the turn of a denominator. The more important missing quantity is the aggregate demand in the relevant local risk hours, which neither annual conversion supplies.

The geographic boundaries are as important as the units. NERC assesses the interconnected bulk system; a state utility commission hears a retail rate case; FERC governs wholesale markets and interstate transmission under federal law; a county may control land use; a city may negotiate water service. None has authority to solve the entire problem alone. A transmission route that benefits several states can face costs that fall unevenly among them. A retail regulator may resist paying for a line whose benefits are mainly outside its territory. A data-center agreement signed locally can alter the regional operator's capacity requirement. The institutional map is therefore part of the electrical diagram.

This fragmentation creates a coordination problem rather than a simple villain. A cautious regulator can be right to demand proof of load before allowing a ratepayer-funded substation. A grid operator can be right to demand a lead time that makes the customer's project uneconomic. A developer can be right that the plant and line it proposes would help the system if they were built together. Each may make a defensible decision within its remit while the whole system moves too slowly. Better stage data, cost-sharing rules, and advance planning help because they allow each actor to see more of the same future. They do not abolish disagreement over who pays.

An instructive way to read an energy headline is to ask five questions before reacting. Is its number energy, power, capacity, a connection request, or a price? Is its geography a neighborhood, a balancing area, an interconnection, the United States, or North America? Is its date an observed past period, a current system condition, a forecast target, or a proposed delivery year? Is it a central scenario, a stress case, or a regulatory requirement? And what happened to the number between proposal and operation? These questions do not settle a policy argument, but they prevent the most common category errors from setting its terms.

They also give the reader a way to update this essay. The exact forecast figures here will age. A new EIA outlook may move annual generation; NERC may change its risk classification; PJM may revise its load forecast; ERCOT may vet its request pipeline. The physical relationships do not expire on the same schedule. A four-hour battery still has a duration, a requested megawatt still needs a delivered connection, and a contract still needs an allocation of cancellation risk. The value of the framework is that it tells us which new observations would genuinely change the conclusion, instead of treating every larger or smaller headline as a reversal.

3. The system is more than its generators#

A plant produces electricity; step-up transformers and high-voltage lines move it; substations reduce voltage; distribution networks bring it to homes and businesses. Markets and regulators sit across these physical layers. In organized wholesale markets, locational prices reflect the marginal cost of serving one more unit of load at a particular place and time, including congestion and losses. A retail bill also recovers distribution, transmission, capacity, policy, and other costs under a tariff. EIA's price primer11 shows why a wholesale price cannot be read as the household price.

Storage, demand response, and transfers are genuine resources, but they answer different problems. A four-hour battery can move daytime solar into an evening peak and provide fast operating services. It cannot create energy during a prolonged shortage unless it can recharge. A flexible data center can reduce a stressed peak, yet its service agreement must make that flexibility real and observable. A new line can diversify supply across weather patterns, but it must be built through land, law, and finance. Every proposed fix must be tied to the time scale and location of the shortage it claims to solve.

Electricity is also sold through several institutional designs. In much of the country, utilities own or contract for resources and recover approved costs through regulated retail rates. Organized wholesale markets, run by regional transmission organizations or independent system operators, dispatch resources and settle energy at location-specific prices. The latter do not replace state retail regulation. A large campus can buy wholesale energy through an intermediary, sign a long-term power-purchase agreement, or locate next to a generator, while still relying on the grid for balancing, backup, or network service. Each arrangement creates a different distribution of risk.

The logic of a locational marginal price is precise. Suppose an additional megawatt of demand can be met by an unconstrained nearby generator at $40/MWh. If the cheap path becomes congested, the next megawatt may require a more expensive local generator or a redispatch elsewhere; the price at the load's node can rise even if the national average cost of fuel has not. Losses add another location-specific component. FERC's market guide12 names energy, congestion, and losses as the components of LMP. That price is a short-run signal for the marginal unit at a given place and time. It is not the full cost of new transmission, a capacity payment, or the final price on a household bill.

This distinction explains why a new data center can have opposite effects in different places. Put a flexible load near curtailed renewable generation, and it may raise the value of output that otherwise would have been wasted. Put a flat load behind a constrained urban interface, and it may raise peak prices and trigger expensive local upgrades. A steady large customer may spread existing fixed costs over more sales, but the benefit disappears if serving it requires an even larger capital program. The correct analysis follows an actual node, tariff, and hour. It cannot be done by multiplying a national average electricity price by a forecasted national data-center TWh total.

New York's public market data show the pattern at regional scale. Across the 8,760 hours from November 2025 through October 2026, the congestion component of NYISO's day-ahead zonal prices13 lowered the West zone's price by $1.96/MWh on average and raised New York City's by $1.74/MWh, while in most hours it was zero in every zone. The averages show where binding constraints separated prices in that year. They do not measure how much headroom a particular substation has, and they cannot tell a developer whether a specific site can connect; that question belongs to a local interconnection study. PJM's Independent Market Monitor14 reports a comparable signal, weighted by load: from January through September 2026, congestion added $25.75/MWh to the Dominion zone's day-ahead price, up from $12.77 in the same months of 2025, while ComEd's component was −$16.74.

The physical and financial systems can also disagree in time. A project may energize before a new line is complete using temporary operating limits. A power-purchase agreement may cover annual energy while the customer still needs capacity at a winter peak. A plant may earn revenue under a capacity contract while a local transmission constraint prevents its output from reaching a particular pocket. Contracts can allocate payments and liability; they do not repeal Kirchhoff's laws. This is why the essay repeatedly asks the same question in different forms: what is available, where, when, and under whose obligation?

The reference essay *Power 2026*15 captures why the electricity story has escaped specialist journals: AI investment has made price formation, power contracts, and fuel choices central to technology strategy. Its wholesale-market primer is a useful entry point. Its more aggressive extrapolations need a different standard of proof. A repeated doubling of data-center electricity use is a scenario that eventually runs into land, chips, equipment, capital, efficiency, and grid limits; it is not a forecast merely because recent growth was rapid. A bundled compute or campus lease converted to dollars per MWh is not the price of wholesale electricity. And a case for coal or diesel as a fast response to scarcity must still compare delivered cost, air pollution, fuel and permit constraints, reliability, and the duration of the need. The accompanying source review traces these distinctions. The lesson is to retain the essay's urgency while testing every arithmetic step against a physical system.

The different clocks also suggest a hierarchy of responses. In hours, operators can dispatch reserves, call demand response, and manage outages. In months, a flexible load contract, line rating equipment, or a new transformer order may help, if site conditions permit. In years, a new plant, major substation, or transmission corridor can change the system's structure. A proposal that solves a 2035 energy problem may be irrelevant to a 2028 connection deadline. A proposal that plugs a 2028 gap with diesel backup may be economically or environmentally poor as a permanent strategy. Keeping the clocks visible helps prevent temporary compromises from becoming unexamined policy.

The problem becomes concrete when a data center sits beside an existing generator. The customer may argue that it will take power directly from the plant and use little grid service. The grid operator must still model the plant's output, the customer's sudden loss or return, transmission use, and the effect on other customers when either facility is unavailable. In a December 2025 PJM order16, FERC found that the existing tariff lacked clear and consistent terms for such co-located loads and directed new options for firm and non-firm contract-demand transmission service, with further compliance filings and a hearing. That order illustrates the central governance issue: a physical location next to a plant does not automatically settle who pays for backup and network capacity.

II. The return of electricity demand#

4. A demand trend changes direction#

U.S. electricity consumption was comparatively flat from the mid-2000s to the early 2020s17. Efficiency gains offset some economic growth. That quiet period shaped utility planning, factory capacity, and regulatory expectations. Now commercial computing, manufacturing, transport, building electrification, and heat compete for more power. In its November 2026 outlook, EIA estimated18 2026 electric-power-sector generation up 2.7% from 2025 and projected another 1.2% increase in 2027. Weather matters to those annual changes; a hot summer can move the total without proving a permanent structural acceleration.

The demand story is uneven. A data-center cluster can alter one utility's load forecast while barely changing the national percentage. An electric vehicle fleet may add substantial annual energy but much less peak demand if charging shifts overnight. Electric heating can create a winter peak at the very hour gas generation also needs fuel. A semiconductor fab brings a different operating profile and water requirements from a server campus. National totals should lead to a map and an hourly load shape, not end the analysis.

The long plateau matters because institutions adapted to it. If a utility expects low growth, it can replace old equipment as needed and buy just enough incremental capacity. Manufacturers then invest in factories sized for a replacement market. Regulators become accustomed to rejecting speculative capital spending. The new load arrives against this inherited pace. It is easy to announce a gigawatt of demand in a quarterly presentation; it is hard to expand a transformer factory, train crews, permit a line, and prove that the customer will actually draw the power. The bottleneck is a mismatch of clocks as much as a shortage of energy.

Even the direction of recent demand is not a straight line. EIA's short-term outlook includes weather, economic activity, fuel prices, and capacity additions, and its forecast changes each month. A single hot year can set a record in annual generation without requiring a new long-term trend line of the same slope. Conversely, a cool year can conceal a permanent industrial load addition. To identify structural demand, compare weather-normalized sales by customer class, connections energized, and the hours in which those customers actually draw power. The published national number is a starting point, not a substitute for these observations.

Electrification also changes the timing of scarcity. An electric car that charges at home after midnight can use output that would otherwise have little value. The same fleet charging on arrival at 6 p.m. could deepen the evening peak. A heat pump can reduce total household energy use while increasing electric demand during a cold spell. Industrial reshoring may introduce steady baseload where a region was accustomed to seasonal variation. These are not arguments against the technologies. They are reminders that an annual TWh forecast cannot answer a capacity question until it is distributed across hours and locations.

The most useful planning map would place new load beside existing transmission capacity, generation, water, land, and local economic conditions. It would then ask where moving the project is cheaper than rebuilding the network. That choice is often ignored in public debate because political boundaries make one site look inevitable. Yet a data center can choose among locations far more easily than a city can move its residents or a factory can move its workforce. The ability to steer flexible investment toward available power is itself an energy resource, provided incentives do not simply shift environmental and ratepayer costs to a less visible community.

5. What the data-center estimates actually say#

Berkeley Lab's June 2026 update19 estimates a 2030 reference case of 649 TWh, bounded by compounded uncertainty cases of 521 and 843 TWh. Its central case is about 74 GW averaged across the year. The report models installed IT equipment, use, lifetimes, and cooling rather than simply adding press releases. It states that 148 GW of interconnection capacity would be needed under an assumed 50% average utilization. That is a modeled connection requirement, not evidence that 148 GW is already built or contracted.

The IEA's April 2026 model20 gives a different U.S. path: 224 TWh in 2025 and 426 TWh in 2030. The gap between 426 and 649 TWh is too large to hide behind a single 'consensus forecast.' Both analyses descend from a bottom-up equipment approach developed by Berkeley Lab, but they use different vintages and inputs. A limited reconciliation separates IT electricity from facility overhead; it does not identify the contribution of each equipment, utilization, or siting assumption to the 223 TWh gap. Neither forecast is measured 2030 demand. The useful common conclusion is narrower: data centers are likely to matter substantially, but their precise U.S. electricity use remains uncertain. The next useful evidence will be actual energized load, equipment deployments, utilization, and regional meter data.

The methodological family resemblance matters. The IEA's Annex A21 explicitly credits Berkeley Lab's bottom-up approach: project shipments of servers, storage and networking equipment, estimate the installed stock and electricity use, then apply facility power usage effectiveness. The IEA allocates a global equipment model among regions using market data and other indicators. Berkeley Lab's U.S. update22 instead refreshed shipment and accelerator inputs available through late 2025, including non-commercial ASICs, newer chip wattages and inference idle-power assumptions. It says its 2025 estimate still contains projections because primary shipment data covered only the first three quarters of that year. Thus even the apparent historical anchor is not a national meter census. The disagreement is better understood as a difference between two model implementations and information sets than as a disagreement between one physical measurement and one opinion.

Berkeley Lab published unusually useful one-at-a-time sensitivity tests. Its 2030 reference case is 649 TWh. A consolidated-deployment case gives 578, a shorter accelerator-lifetime case 590, a higher specialized-chip case 664, and a higher inference-energy case 782 TWh. The high inference case alone is 133 TWh above reference, about 15.2 GW of constant power. Those alternatives do not allocate the entire IEA gap, because they are perturbations within Berkeley Lab's own model. They do show that utilization and idle draw deserve at least as much scrutiny as the number of announced campuses. A planner trying to reconcile the two institutions would need their shipment cohort tables, equipment power curves, retirement assumptions, facility shares and PUE paths on the same calendar and geographic basis. Without that bridge, the 223 TWh difference is a model-risk signal, not a ranked verdict on which institution is right.

There is, however, an accounting bridge in the public tables. The IEA reports23 345 TWh of 2030 U.S. IT electricity within its 426 TWh facility total. Berkeley Lab reports24 an aggregate 1.36 facility power usage effectiveness, so its 649 TWh total implies roughly 477 TWh of IT electricity. If one holds the IEA's IT energy fixed and applies Berkeley Lab's PUE, the total becomes about 469 TWh: roughly 43 TWh of the gap appears in that first facility-efficiency step, and 180 TWh remains in the implied IT-energy step. Reverse the order, and those steps become roughly 60 and 163 TWh. These are order-dependent arithmetic allocations, not proof that cooling caused a particular fraction of the forecast gap. The reconciliation note shows the derivation and the unresolved inputs. In 2024, the two reports' U.S. estimates differed by only 9 TWh; most of the 2030 divergence is in projected growth.

The interaction with the grid is another unresolved layer. Berkeley Lab explicitly says it does not model a large change in trajectory caused by grid power availability or supply-chain constraints. Its 649 TWh is therefore a consumption scenario conditional on equipment and facility assumptions, not a guarantee that the grid can deliver that electricity on schedule. A utility's request inventory can be larger than realized demand while the equipment model is still internally coherent. Conversely, a delayed interconnection may displace a facility to an onsite plant or another region rather than remove compute demand entirely. A future data set that joins equipment deliveries, signed power agreements and actual regional load would test both forecasts more directly than another national forecast curve.

Berkeley Lab's estimate deserves a closer look because its precision is visually seductive. The reference case is the output of assumptions about how many servers are sold, how long they operate, how much electricity the IT equipment uses, and how much additional energy goes to cooling and power distribution. The report distinguishes conventional and AI-related equipment but cannot observe every privately built facility's future utilization. Its uncertainty cases compound different plausible assumptions; 521 and 843 TWh are not confidence bounds with an agreed statistical coverage. The spread is a feature of the work, not an embarrassment to hide in a footnote. The full report25 also provides sensitivity cases that move the 2030 total materially when the central equipment and efficiency assumptions change.

The IEA's 426 TWh U.S. base case and Berkeley Lab's 649 TWh reference case differ by 223 TWh, equivalent to roughly 25 GW of constant demand. This is larger than the output of many substantial generating fleets. Yet it would be a mistake to take their midpoint and call it truth. A difference in baseline scope or equipment assumptions would not disappear through averaging. Instead, a planner should keep both paths visible and run a decision through each. Does a proposed line still pay off under the low path? Can a transformer order be staged? What is the cost of a service delay under the high path? A forecast is useful if it changes a decision for the right reason, not if it produces a reassuringly neat number.

There is also a distinction between efficiency per computation and electricity consumption in total. Better accelerators can perform more operations per joule, while cheaper computation invites more training runs, larger models, more inference, and new products. Power usage effectiveness, or PUE, measures total facility energy divided by IT equipment energy. Improving PUE lowers overhead for a given IT load, but it does not determine how much IT load the market will deploy. Nor does the energy use of a single inference request settle the energy demand of an entire software ecosystem. The IEA's April assessment26 emphasizes that efficiency gains and growing use can occur together. The direction of the net effect is empirical.

AI-specific energy estimates are particularly sensitive to utilization. A provisioned GPU cluster may not run at rated power throughout its life. Training, inference, networking, storage, and cooling have different profiles, and a new installation ramps as hardware, users, and software arrive. Power supply design must accommodate a peak or credible ramp, while an energy model averages across idle and active periods. If a developer reserves 1 GW for a campus but builds 250 MW of first-phase equipment, its current grid impact is different from the eventual rights embedded in its connection agreement. The distinction matters to both system planning and who pays for reserved capacity.

Longer-term forecasts have a second trap: they can be internally inconsistent with the constraints they claim to measure. A model might project AI adoption fast enough to require 100 GW of new grid connections by 2030 while also assuming the chips, buildings, transformers, and transmission that enable those connections arrive on time. If one of those inputs is binding, demand cannot materialize on that timetable. The unmet desire for power is economically consequential, but it is not metered electricity. A credible forecast should publish both unconstrained demand and the likely realized path under equipment and grid delays. The difference is an estimate of deferred or displaced activity, with uncertainty, rather than hidden in a single curve.

6. The megawatt that exists only on paper#

Connection applications are options on future grid capacity. A developer can explore multiple sites, request more power than the first phase will use, or abandon a project when financing or chips change. A utility must still plan for credible customers. The resulting error can run in either direction: overbuilding for phantom demand can burden other ratepayers; dismissing genuine projects can leave a region short of infrastructure.

Texas exposes the difference. An ERCOT briefing in February 202627 tracked roughly 232 GW of large-load requests seeking connection, with stages ranging from no studies submitted to observed energization. That number cannot be inserted directly into a demand forecast. In May, ERCOT proposed a realization-rate adjustment28 to its preliminary long-term forecast, an acknowledgment that applications need a probability of completion. The best public load ledger would separately show requested, studied, financially secured, under construction, energized, and measured operating load. It would publish attrition by project stage and cohort without exposing sensitive customer information.

ERCOT's own sequence shows why this ledger matters. In February, its planning material listed roughly 232.5 GW of large-load requests for 2030, with about 6.9 GW observed energized in the 2025 snapshot. A March status file showed 410.6 GW of requests for 2030, about 87.6% attributed to data centers. Of that March total, 293.7 GW had no studies submitted, 86.6 GW was under review, 21.3 GW had approved planning studies, 3.2 GW was approved to energize but not operating, and 5.8 GW was observed energized. These categories are not equal promises of future consumption. The totals also changed across snapshots and definitions, so the difference between February and March is not a measured increase in demand. ERCOT's March briefing29 supplies the categories and the caveat a headline usually omits.

The problem became unmistakable when ERCOT's April preliminary forecast30 projected a 2032 summer peak of 367.79 GW against a historical peak near 85.5 GW. ERCOT acknowledged that the preliminary method likely overstated the share of proposed loads that would materialize and sought a way to adjust. A projection generated from unfiltered applications is useful as an upper planning signal, but it is not a plausible central trajectory simply because a grid operator printed it. The appropriate lesson is neither to scoff at all data-center demand nor to assume Texas must quadruple its system in six years. It is to measure conversion from application to operation by stage, customer type, and time since request.

There is a useful numerical experiment inside ERCOT's own preliminary presentation31. Its 2030 core forecast, before adding the new medium- and large-load submissions, is 107.287 GW. With those submissions, the displayed peak becomes 319.650 GW. The 212.363 GW difference is the effect of adding reported proposed loads to that particular method. It is not a measured surge, and it cannot be compared directly with a completed generation fleet. ERCOT's December 2025 capacity report32 had a 2030 adjusted summer peak of 138.010 GW, using an earlier vintage and a different set of inclusion rules. The three figures are best shown as labeled planning lenses: base growth, adjusted projects, and an unfiltered request-sensitive preliminary case. Averaging them would produce a number with no operator behind it.

The December report also provides a harder warning than the huge April headline. In its base case, with protocol-prescribed capacity resources and its adjusted load forecast, summer and winter planning reserve margins fall and turn negative in 2028. This is conditional on the load and resource assumptions, and the report contains alternative cases for large-load curtailment. A negative modeled margin is an instruction to test timing, new supply and verified flexibility, not a precise prediction of when ERCOT will shed load. The right follow-up is cohort evidence: of the applications expected to energize by March 2026, what fraction did, at what first-phase MW, and how fast did actual imports ramp? ERCOT proposed historical realization adjustments33 for just this reason. Those conversion rates should be updated by load type and stage. Until they are public and stable, a single realization multiplier applied to 410.6 GW is false precision.

PJM has a different data problem. It does not maintain a formal queue for large-load interconnection34. Its load-serving entities submit adjustments to the load forecast. A November 2026 PJM filing35 reported 61.322 GW of requested 2030 load adjustments as of September, compared with 33.707 GW incorporated in the 2026 forecast. The difference is unvetted proposed load, not a 27.615 GW discovery of new demand. The next forecast can add, reduce, defer, or reject requests after vetting. This is why an auction shortage calculated against one forecast vintage should not be silently paired with a later, larger request list.

The accounting problem has a strategic dimension. A developer that believes a competitor may reserve a scarce substation has an incentive to request early and generously. The grid operator sees the sum of everyone's options; the individual projects may all believe only a fraction will be built. Raising financial-readiness requirements can reduce this inflation, but it can also exclude smaller credible entrants. Milestones, deposits that grow with project maturity, transparent attrition statistics, and the ability to release unused reservation are ways to test seriousness without claiming certainty. The IEA's October 2026 survey36 describes the same queue-inflation problem across jurisdictions.

The electricity system also needs a language for deferred demand. If a customer cannot connect until 2032, a 2030 regional sales forecast should not count its full proposed load. An economic-development analysis may still count a lost opportunity. A public dashboard should report both, separately. This would reveal whether a policy improved actual power delivery or merely made the forecast look smaller by rejecting requests. Conversely, a region that overbuilds for applications that never reach operation has made an expensive mistake even if it can boast generous capacity. Honest planning must track false positives and false negatives over time.

III. Why America cannot simply build more power#

7. Build the right resource for the hour#

The resource mix is expanding, but installed megawatts are not interchangeable. EIA reported in February 202637 that developers then planned a record 86 GW of U.S. utility-scale capacity additions that year, dominated by solar and batteries. These were plans, not completed plants; a solar GW's contribution during a winter evening differs from a gas unit's, and a battery's depends on its state of charge and duration. Conversely, a dispatchable plant can fail during extreme weather or lack firm fuel. Reliability analysis must model correlated weather, outages, fuel, imports, and the changing risk hour rather than assign permanent 'good' and 'bad' labels to technologies.

Solar and storage have shorter construction cycles and are already improving some seasonal margins. NERC's summer 2026 assessment38 found anticipated resources up from 1,115 to 1,173 GW and fewer regions at elevated risk than in 2025. That is an important correction to a one-way collapse narrative. It remains a conditional seasonal assessment. Accelerated loads, prolonged low renewable output, and extreme weather can still break a comfortable-looking reserve calculation.

PJM offers a concrete test of what 'enough' means. Its 2028–29 capacity auction procured 138.318 GW through its market and counted another 10.864 GW of resources under separate Fixed Resource Requirement arrangements. The combined 149.182 GW was 6.831 GW below the 156.013 GW reliability requirement. The auction report39 explicitly warns against interpreting the shortfall as an inability to serve load. It signals a higher modeled probability of needing emergency measures under adverse conditions. The price reached the auction's $325 per unforced-capacity MW-day cap. The total reported auction value of about $16.4 billion is a market procurement measure, not a line item that can be divided by households to obtain their bill increase; bilateral obligations, self-supply, and retail arrangements intervene.

The next analytical step is to ask why the margin narrowed. An increase in the forecast peak raises the requirement. Retirements, outages, or revised accreditation lower dependable supply. A ceiling on the auction price can leave the market unable to clear the full reliability target even if customers would value more reserves. Demand response and imports can contribute, but only to the degree they are accredited and deliverable. PJM's January 2026 forecast anticipated 183.008 GW of summer peak load in 2030 and 216.872 GW in 2035; it also lowered near-term expectations compared with the previous forecast after reexamining electric vehicles, the economy, and large loads. PJM's forecast release40 shows that 'load growth is accelerating' and 'the forecast has been revised down' can both describe the same planning cycle.

Capacity markets do not build plants by declaration. A resource has to survive financing, construction, testing, and interconnection. Nor is an auction the sole reliability tool. Operators can call reserves, seek emergency purchases, request conservation, or, at the far end of an escalating sequence, shed load. These options have different costs and limits. A slim capacity margin raises the value of preparation and the cost of forecast error. It should prompt examination of fuel assurance, transmission delivery, flexible load, and project milestones, rather than a confident date for a blackout that the report itself does not supply.

The auction arithmetic is worth holding still. The 6.8314 GW difference is between PJM's accredited, procured capacity and a reliability requirement for a delivery year. It is not 6.8314 GW of load already unserved, and it is not 6.8314 GW of new power plants whose nameplates would settle the matter. A new solar or battery project would receive an accredited value based on modeled contribution during risk hours, not its full nameplate. An existing unit can be counted differently after performance assumptions change. PJM's 2026 ELCC explanation41 says the contribution depends on the portfolio and the hours of tight operation. To move from a margin to a risk estimate, one needs a distribution of weather, outages and deliverable imports. The methods companion works through that distinction with an exact small example.

Large-load service is being negotiated on a separate clock. FERC's December 2025 order on PJM co-location42 found that the tariff lacked clear terms for generators serving colocated load and directed PJM to develop firm and non-firm service options. The order did not itself create a universal, finished tariff for every data center. The service choice affects what the campus may import when its adjacent generator fails, how network facilities are studied, and what capacity and cost obligations remain. Those rules are as consequential as the auction price. A campus that appears “off grid” in a diagram can still require firm backup service when its plant trips. A rule that allocates no cost to that contingency risks shifting an expensive option to other customers. A rule that charges for a contingency the grid cannot actually provide would make an equally hollow promise.

The right portfolio also changes with weather and the existing system. A battery paired with solar may improve a summer evening margin but contribute less during a multi-day winter shortage unless it can recharge. Wind output may be strongest in winter, yet a regional weather pattern can suppress it over a wide area. Gas can run through dark, still hours if fuel is available; freezing equipment and pipeline constraints can create a correlated failure. Diversification helps when failure modes are genuinely different. Buying five resources that share one gas pipeline or one transmission corridor is less diversified than their technology labels suggest.

The long-term picture also has an important near-term qualification. NERC's 2025 assessment43 says approved on-peak resource additions in integrated plans can keep pace with aggregated peak-demand forecasts through 2028. Beyond that point, new approvals and successful completion of less mature projects become more important. The statement is contingent on facilities arriving as planned and on the forecasts being reasonably accurate; it is neither an all-clear nor proof of an immediate nationwide shortage. In the most certain Tier 1 category, NERC estimates roughly 60 GW of net additions over ten years after retirements. It says another roughly 190 GW of Tier 2 or other resources would need to mature to meet expected growth. A reader should see both halves of that assessment before concluding that the grid is already out of power or that the build pipeline is sufficient.

8. Firm fuel has constraints too#

Natural gas offers controllable generation and an existing U.S. fuel base. It also binds electricity reliability to gas production, pipelines, compressor stations, and contracting. During prolonged cold, heating demand and power generation can compete for the same fuel while infrastructure itself is stressed. The joint FERC–NERC review of winter 202644 found the electric and gas systems operated without major incident, with no reported manual electric load shedding, while describing significant operational constraints and the role of advance coordination. That is evidence that preparation works and that close calls deserve study.

Nuclear provides high annual output but new plants have long development schedules and large financing exposure. Existing reactors can be valuable if their operation is safe and economic. Hydro is flexible where geography permits, but drought changes its output. Geothermal is promising where geology, drilling, and resource confirmation align; its national scale and timing should be demonstrated, not assumed. Wind and solar can be built rapidly but require transmission, balancing resources, and attention to coincident weather. Coal and diesel can supply near-term energy in some cases, but their fuel, emissions, cost, and local harms are real constraints. A serious portfolio weighs construction time, reliability contribution, delivered cost, emissions, and the ability to operate through the specific stressed hours.

Reliability and carbon constraints need to be examined together rather than treated as rival slogans. Keeping a high-emitting unit temporarily available can reduce near-term reliability risk, while building it into a decades-long growth plan can lock in fuel and emissions exposure. A new zero-carbon plant can be attractive for long-run energy but too late for a 2028 connection. Storage can improve peak service while needing clean or reliable recharge energy. The relevant comparison is a portfolio over time, including construction milestones, fuel contracts, emissions, and the value of avoiding interruption. A single levelized cost per MWh omits location, timing, system services, and the cost of integration.

The winter 2026 review is instructive precisely because it was not a catastrophe. FERC and NERC45 describe stress in gas and electric operations, but coordinated advance preparation and communication kept the system operating without reported manual electric load shedding. The conclusion is not that gas risk is imaginary. It is that operating protocols, fuel arrangements, and information exchange can change outcomes without adding a single gigawatt of nameplate capacity. Any plan that counts a gas plant as firm should specify the circumstances in which it has fuel, whether its pipeline is shared with other units, and how it is maintained in cold weather.

Onsite generation should receive the same scrutiny. A data-center operator may wish to bypass a long grid queue by building its own gas plant. The facility then needs turbines or engines, fuel, air permits, cooling, maintenance, backup units, and a way to start and stabilize the system. If it retains a grid connection for backup, the grid may have to plan for the campus's full load just when the onsite plant fails. If it does not, the campus carries a higher interruption risk itself. The IEA's April 2026 analysis46 notes the overbuild required for reliable onsite gas supply. An onsite solution can be rational for a particular customer; it cannot be counted as a free national supply addition without the associated fuel and reserve assumptions.

The less dramatic option is to buy time by retiring plants carefully and using existing facilities better. A retirement delay can protect a short planning window if the unit is physically sound, fuel-secure, and its costs are transparent. It can also obscure the need to commission replacement capacity. Uprates at existing plants, heat-rate improvements, and better maintenance may provide incremental help faster than greenfield construction, but their potential is site-specific. The metric is additional dependable power at the relevant hour, with a dated retirement or replacement path. A press release about 'preserving baseload' is not an adequacy study.

9. A queue is not a power plant#

Berkeley Lab's 2026 queue survey47 counted roughly 1,312 GW of proposed generation and 749 GW of storage actively seeking U.S. transmission interconnection at the end of 2025. It covers generator and storage queues, not data-center load requests. The stocks are enormous, yet only 13% of capacity that entered queues from 2000 through 2020 had reached commercial operation by the end of 2025; 75% had withdrawn. That historical conversion rate should not be mechanically applied to every new cohort, but it defeats the idea that queue capacity equals forthcoming supply.

The same Berkeley Lab analysis found the median time from interconnection request to commercial operation for projects built in 2025 exceeded five years in regions with data. Grid studies, upgrade costs, equipment, siting, and financing all intervene. FERC Order 202348 moved toward cluster studies and stronger readiness requirements. Its full effect is still too early to measure. The question for each region is how many projects have completed studies, secured equipment and permits, and can deliver dependable output by the date a large load actually arrives.

The queue's scale is a sign of investment interest and a sign of institutional congestion. At the end of 2025, Berkeley Lab counted49 more than 8,200 active generation and storage projects. About 549 GW of projects had a draft or executed interconnection agreement but had not reached commercial operation. An agreement therefore narrows uncertainty but does not erase construction, procurement, or financing risk. The median request-to-operation time for projects completed in 2025 exceeded five years in regions with data. This historical statistic is not a promise that a new project will take exactly five years; it is a warning against solving a 2028 load shortage by pointing to an unstudied 2026 application.

Why can so much proposed supply coexist with a short reserve margin? The answer is not a single bureaucratic failure. Projects cluster in attractive locations, where the existing transmission network may already be saturated. A study can find that a small project's connection requires a much larger network upgrade. That upgrade must be assigned a payer, engineered, permitted, supplied with equipment, and synchronized with neighboring projects whose plans may change. A developer may withdraw when a cost estimate rises. The next developer then enters a changed queue. FERC's cluster-study reform attempts to study related projects together and require stronger commercial readiness. The outcome should be judged by completed facilities and lower uncertainty, not by the number of applications processed.

There is a common misunderstanding in the word 'interconnection.' Generator interconnection studies ask whether a plant can safely inject power and what upgrades that injection needs. They do not necessarily prove that its output can travel without congestion to every load that wants it. A large customer connection asks a different question: can the system serve a withdrawal with the promised reliability and tariff terms? The two queues may be governed by different entities and rules, which is why subtracting a national generator queue from a national data-center request list is meaningless. The relevant calculation is regional, hourly, and network-constrained.

There are near-term ways to get more from existing wires. Dynamic line ratings can adjust a line's safe transfer limit as ambient conditions and wind change. Advanced power-flow controls can steer flows around congestion. Better topology optimization can identify safe switching arrangements. The IEA's September 2026 grid modernization report50 surveys these tools as a way to improve use of existing networks. None turns a 100 MW line into an unlimited corridor; each requires measurement, protection coordination, operator trust, and verification of its value under the conditions that matter. They should be evaluated alongside new lines, not as a talisman for avoiding construction.

The long-run transmission case is broader. Building across regions can let one area's wind, solar, hydro, or conventional fleet support another during a different weather pattern. The benefit depends on correlation: if both regions suffer the same extreme event, the transfer may be unavailable or too small. A line also creates economic value by moving cheaper output to expensive locations in ordinary hours. DOE's July 2026 draft needs study51 identifies congestion and interregional transfer limitations, while FERC Order 192052 sets a framework for longer-term regional planning and cost allocation. Both are steps in a slow physical process. A plan does not energize steel, land rights, or transformers.

10. Transformers make the abstract shortage tangible#

The grid's least glamorous components can be its longest lead items. DOE reported in October 202653 multiyear transformer lead times and estimated that imports supply about 80% of U.S. power transformers and 50% of distribution transformers. A replacement is not a generic object on a warehouse shelf. Voltage, impedance, physical dimensions, cooling, transport, and protection interfaces can be site specific. The large units are expensive to fabricate and move.

These facts do not establish that a single attack could disable the entire country for years. Networks have spare capacity, alternative paths, mutual assistance, and restoration procedures, though these vary by location and event. They do establish a shared constraint: new substations, ordinary replacements, storm recovery, and security reserves all draw from the same equipment supply chain. Standardizing specifications where feasible, expanding domestic manufacturing, qualifying multiple suppliers, and holding strategic spares are unglamorous forms of resilience. The precise cost and lead-time benefit of each requires utility-level data that is often not public.

Transformer scarcity has several causes. Demand is rising for new data centers, renewable connections, distribution growth, and routine replacement at once. Large transformers are specialized and expensive; factories need skilled labor and high-grade electrical steel, copper, and insulation components. International supply chains can be efficient in normal times but harder to surge after multiple disasters. Utilities also specify different designs, some for sound engineering reasons and some through inherited practice. Standardization can improve substitutability, yet a rushed universal design could create new common-mode weaknesses or fail to fit an existing substation. The useful target is a smaller family of qualified designs with clear exceptions.

A transformer lead time is not the same as an outage restoration time. Operators can reroute power, install mobile units, draw on spare inventories, or make temporary repairs after a failure. The feasibility depends on the failed unit and network geometry. It is therefore misleading to say that a 30-month factory lead time implies a 30-month blackout. It is equally misleading to assume a mutual-aid agreement can replace several unusual high-voltage units after a coordinated regional disaster. A public resilience assessment should report categories of spare compatibility and realistic restoration sequences without publishing a targeting manual.

The cost of waiting is often hidden in the capital budget. A project may have financing, land, and servers before its substation is ready. The economic loss is then the value of delayed service, which can be much larger than the transformer's purchase price. But using that delay to justify paying any amount for emergency equipment would invite waste and transfer costs to customers. A sound procurement strategy places early orders against a credible portfolio of projects, uses deposits to screen speculative load, and creates options to redirect equipment if one customer cancels. This is easier to say than to contract: ownership, specifications, warranties, and rate recovery all have to be settled before a spare can move.

The supply chain also reaches beyond transformers. Circuit breakers, switchgear, protection relays, turbines, cables, and skilled installers can each determine the critical path. DOE's October 2026 equipment assessment54 should be read as evidence of broad pressure rather than as a universal schedule for every item. The practical question for a proposed 2029 campus is a bill of materials with vendor reservations and delivery contingencies. If any one component has no credible source, the advertised grid-connection date is a hope.

IV. An infrastructure built to be reliable, not invulnerable#

11. Cybersecurity is an operational discipline#

Digital control makes a large electric system manageable, and creates attack surfaces. The most credible questions concern access to operational technology, vendor software, remote connections, identity systems, and the ability to detect and contain compromise. The bulk-power system is subject to mandatory reliability standards, but distribution utilities and customer equipment inhabit a different regulatory landscape. A cyber intrusion can be serious without producing a blackout; conversely, loss of visibility or control during an emergency may compound another disturbance.

Public evidence does not justify a confident probability for a coordinated national cyber outage. It justifies investment in segmentation, tested recovery, supply-chain scrutiny, and exercises that include gas, communications, and local distribution. The GAO's electricity cybersecurity review55 documents governance gaps and tracks recommendations, but its original 2019 findings should not be recast as a 2026 incident count. Security work also requires discretion: an essay can explain failure modes and institutional responsibilities without publishing exploitable site-specific details.

The distinction between a cyber incident and a grid outage is especially important when reading public reports. An intrusion into business IT can expose customer data or impair billing without reaching operational control. A compromise of a vendor may create a path toward operational equipment but still be contained. Conversely, ransomware that does not directly control a breaker can slow dispatch, field work, or restoration. Counting every attempted intrusion as a blackout risk exaggerates; counting only blackouts misses near misses and institutional fragility. The evidence needed for a national probability estimate is scarce, classified, and heterogeneous. The honest public claim is about failure modes and defenses, not a numerical doomsday clock.

Electric utilities also face an awkward security boundary at the customer connection. A very large data center may have sophisticated on-site protection, power electronics, and backup generation, while the grid operator has limited visibility into exactly how those systems respond to a voltage dip. A customer might independently decide to switch to backup supply, suddenly removing gigawatts of load from the bulk system. The IEA's October report56 notes an abrupt U.S. disconnection of more than 3 GW of data-center load. That event is an operational performance question, not an allegation of attack. It shows how a privately controlled facility can become a system-scale electrical actor.

Standards have to follow the electrical consequence, not only the familiar labels of generator and load. NERC's May 2026 computational-load alert57 called for verified load models, commissioning studies, disturbance recording, and direct communication. On October 26, NERC's Board adopted three foundational computational-load standards covering interconnection studies and models, operational data and communications, and protection coordination and disturbance monitoring. NERC says it filed them with FERC on November 1658. Board adoption and a petition are not FERC approval or an effective date. If approved, the requirements could help operators predict whether a campus rides through a fault, trips, or changes consumption abruptly. They should be paired with sensible confidentiality: operators need actionable technical information, but publishing exact control thresholds or recovery weaknesses may create risk. The resulting public record should show compliance, event analysis, and improvements without becoming an adversary's manual.

Cyber resilience is finally a recovery problem. A utility can assume some detection will fail and still limit consequences by separating networks, preserving offline backups of configurations, testing manual operation, and rehearsing communications with neighboring utilities. The operational test is not whether a company can display a long cybersecurity policy. It is whether people can identify bad telemetry, isolate affected systems, maintain safe power flows, and restore trustworthy control before a separate contingency arrives. The same exercise should include third-party cloud services and gas-sector dependencies where relevant. A security program that never tests the full chain may be compliant yet brittle.

12. Physical attack is different from system collapse#

Substations, lines, control centers, and communications sites can be damaged or disrupted. The consequences depend on location, redundancy, repair stock, operator response, and whether an attacker can coordinate multiple events. A visible local outage tells us little about the probability of a continental cascade. The distinction between bulk transmission and the distribution system serving a neighborhood matters here, as it does for storm statistics.

FERC approved the revised CIP-014-4 physical-security standard in September 202659. It is aimed at identifying and protecting critical transmission facilities through more consistent analysis. That action is evidence that the risk is taken seriously, not evidence that every critical asset is exposed. A useful security dashboard would track consequential disruptions, restoration time, tested spare capacity, and exercises, while withholding specific defensive layouts. The relevant public question is whether the system can absorb and recover from a plausible regional event, not whether a dramatic worst-case story can be imagined.

Physical security has to balance two kinds of error. Failing to protect a uniquely consequential facility can expose many customers. Declaring every substation equally critical can spread limited money too thin and obscure which sites truly need engineered barriers or spare transformers. CIP-014 is aimed at high-consequence transmission stations and control centers, not every distribution cabinet. Its revised 2026 version improves the assessment framework, but a standard's existence does not prove implementation quality at every site. Regulators need audits and exercises; the public needs aggregate results and accountability.

The most plausible physical disruptions are often local or regional. A damaged distribution feeder can cut service to a neighborhood even if national generation is abundant. A storm can bring down many feeders at once, making the availability of crews and replacement equipment more important than the reserve margin in a wholesale auction. Conversely, damaging one high-voltage asset may have little customer impact if the network has alternative paths. This variability frustrates a single national 'grid vulnerability' score. The proper unit of analysis is the network around a critical facility, its backup paths, and restoration sequence.

Security and reliability investments can reinforce each other. A spare transformer, redundant communications path, or better physical separation can improve storm recovery as well as resistance to attack. Some investments create tradeoffs: a heavily centralized control system may simplify coordination but create a concentrated target; decentralization may improve survival while complicating visibility and dispatch. There is no universal rule that every component should be hardened maximally. A risk-based plan should ask which failure combinations would cause the most unserved energy, which can be prevented, and which can be recovered from quickly.

This is where threat storytelling becomes least helpful. It is possible to imagine an adversary timing several attacks during a heat wave. It is not possible, from public headlines alone, to derive the probability or duration of nationwide failure. The essay's security argument is modest and testable: long equipment lead times, concentrated facilities, shared software, and correlated hazards warrant preparation; actual operational history and restoration capability should discipline the scale of the claim. Panic can divert attention from the boring measures most likely to shorten a real outage.

13. Weather and recovery are the recurring test#

Storms, fire, flooding, heat, drought, and extreme cold can damage assets and raise demand simultaneously. They can also produce correlated generator outages, especially when a common fuel or cooling system fails. Black-start plans, restoration crews, mutual aid, and pre-positioned equipment determine how a region recovers after a large disturbance. Resilience is therefore a time profile: what fails, how much load is lost, and how quickly service returns.

The evidence cuts both ways. NERC's 2026 State of Reliability60 reviews 2025 bulk-system performance and emerging challenges. The joint winter 2026 review61 records strong performance under severe cold alongside substantial stress. Both should be read as evidence about adaptive institutions, not an all-clear for every future storm. A credible assessment compares emergency declarations, actual customer interruption, unserved energy, restoration time, and near misses rather than counting every alarming incident as a failure of equal severity.

The January and February 2026 cold period gives this distinction a real date. The FERC, NERC and regional-entity staff review62 covered the Eastern and Texas interconnections during storms Fern and Gianna. It reported no manual electric load shedding by the entities in its scope and no widespread loss of firm natural-gas service. Yet total U.S. electric demand, a lower-48 figure from EIA data, reached 666 GW on January 27, and generator outages across the Eastern and Texas interconnections, excluding the Southeastern reliability coordinator's area, peaked at 68.169 GW the day before. Those are different measures on different days; subtracting the outage peak from the demand peak would invent a simultaneous balance that the report does not supply. The system met the challenge while carrying material stress.

The report calls the period an endurance challenge. Across what it calls an eleven-day window around the peak, it records 138.8 TWh of lower-48 electric demand; in EIA's hourly data that total corresponds to the 241 hours from five days before to five days after the peak hour, January 22 to February 1. Operators had to manage equipment limits, fuel procurement, unit commitment, transfers and changing load forecasts over days rather than cover a single peak hour. Emergency federal orders allowed dispatch of resources that otherwise would not have been available. Interregional transfers helped, but the staff cautioned that the same transfer capability might not be available in another storm. In one notable episode, a MISO price-publishing tool failed for about 12 hours and cut imports; neighboring systems agreed to raise MISO's internal south-to-north transfer limit to a peak of 4,200 MW while TVA monitored thermal and voltage limits. Separately, at its hour of largest net inflow during the storm, MISO imported about 9 GW from its neighbors. This is not evidence of unlimited import capacity. It is evidence that coordination and transmission have real value and real boundaries.

Gas and electricity were coupled in less visible ways. New England used LNG and fuel oil when pipeline deliverability was constrained; LNG helped relieve pressure on gas supplies available to New York generators. Fuel oil in turn created a need for non-fuel consumables and delivery logistics. The analysis warns that a generator with no firm gas transportation can be disadvantaged when pipelines fill. It also describes weather and behavioral forecast errors: school closures, distribution outages and changing travel patterns altered expected load, affecting fuel and unit commitments. A forecasting error in one area can therefore change fuel availability elsewhere. A reliability model that treats each region and fuel plant as independent misses the mechanism.

It is tempting to turn the no-shed result into proof that winterization is finished. The report instead credits preparation, generator commitments, better situational awareness, cold-weather standards and communication, while documenting remaining fuel and equipment constraints. This is the strongest anti-doomer evidence in the essay because it is an observed stressful period with a successful bulk-system outcome. It is also a warning against complacency: the result depended on deliberate preparation and an operating toolbox that may face a different configuration next time. The appropriate public scorecard would track manual shedding, emergency actions, outage causes, fuel inventories, transfer limits and restoration in subsequent winters, using consistent definitions.

Extreme weather is the grid's repeated natural experiment. It reveals whether equipment is maintained, fuel can arrive, operators can coordinate across jurisdictions, and forecasts capture the tail of the distribution. But the experiment is not perfectly controlled. An uneventful storm may reflect a milder actual hazard, an unusually high reserve, advance conservation, or a successful emergency response. A damaging storm may expose a weakness that was invisible in normal operations. After-action reports are valuable when they separate those causes and lead to verifiable changes.

The bulk-power system and the distribution system can tell opposite stories during one event. High-voltage generation and transmission may stay stable while trees, ice, or flooding disable thousands of local feeders. A headline stating that 'the grid failed' may refer to widespread household outages even though the regional operator maintained frequency and reserve. Both matter to people, but they demand different remedies. Burying distribution lines, trimming vegetation, elevating equipment, hardening poles, and deploying local backup are evaluated against specific hazards and costs. Adding a remote power plant would not repair a washed-out feeder. January 2025 shows the split in data. The joint review reports no manual load shedding during that month's arctic events, yet ORNL's EAGLE-I outage records63 show 410,289 customers without power at one point on January 6 in states outside the Western Interconnection. None of those outages came from operators rationing bulk-system supply.

Recovery deserves its own metrics. Customer interruptions count people affected, while unserved energy integrates magnitude and duration. A brief interruption to a million customers and a three-day outage to a small town have different social consequences even if a single outage count merges them. Critical facilities such as hospitals, water systems, and communications hubs add another dimension. Useful public reporting would show restoration curves, not only the initial number of customers out, and explain whether failures came from generation, transmission, distribution, fuel, or communication. It would also compare forecasted restoration time with actual performance.

Climate change complicates use of history. A system designed from twentieth-century weather records may face different heat, fire, flood, and precipitation patterns. The exact local shift remains uncertain, and not every event can be attributed in the same way. Planners can still stress-test plausible combinations: high temperature and low wind, prolonged cold and gas constraints, or wildfire smoke and multiple line outages. The goal is to discover thresholds where small changes produce large service losses. Those thresholds justify targeted adaptation better than a generic declaration that every risk is unprecedented.

Finally, resilience can be bought from the demand side. A hospital with tested backup, a water utility with storage, or a data center that can reduce noncritical work can lower the consequences of a grid event. But backup must be maintained, fueled, and regularly exercised. A generator that runs for one hour during a demonstration does not prove a three-day fuel plan. Nor should private backup be treated as a reason to neglect public service for households that cannot afford it. The fair question is which investments protect broad service and which transfer outage costs to people with less capacity to adapt.

V. The new political economy of power#

14. The bill has several authors#

An electric bill recovers energy, generation capacity, transmission, distribution, and other charges, depending on jurisdiction. In 2025 the EIA's national average residential price64 was 17.30 cents per kWh, with wide state variation. A rise in that average cannot be attributed wholesale to data centers. Fuel prices, weather, aging equipment, storm hardening, policy, and rate-case timing also matter. The relevant causal question is narrower: what incremental costs does a new load trigger, who initially finances them, and which customers ultimately bear them if the project grows, shrinks, or disappears?

Wholesale locational marginal prices can rise when a new load strains a constrained hour. They can also fall in some hours if new, steady demand supports capacity investment or uses otherwise curtailed energy. The transmission and distribution bill may move differently from the energy price. A claim that data centers always raise or always lower bills evades the tariff, place, load shape, and timing that decide the result.

The phrase 'electricity price' conceals at least four different prices. A wholesale energy price pays the marginal unit in a particular hour and location. A capacity payment pays for accredited availability in a future delivery period where a capacity market exists. A utility's retail rate recovers an approved portfolio of costs, including distribution assets that are invisible in a wholesale price chart. A large customer's negotiated contract may allocate risk in a way that never appears in the posted household rate. Mixing these prices can make a dramatic but meaningless comparison. EIA's 2025 customer-class averages65 illustrate the point: 17.30 cents per kWh for residential customers, 13.41 for commercial, and 8.62 for industrial. Those differences reflect load shape, service voltage, cost allocation, and tariff design, not simply a discount for using more electrons.

The claim that AI will raise household bills should be tested against a counterfactual. Which plants, lines, and substations would have been built without the new load? Which fuel or capacity prices changed because of it? Did the customer pay a dedicated charge or deposit? Did its purchases spread the fixed cost of existing assets over more sales? Did its presence accelerate a project that also benefits other customers? A rate case may answer some of these questions, but it often arrives years after an investment decision. Analysts should distinguish the immediate wholesale effect from the eventual retail allocation. A higher wholesale price during a few scarce hours may coexist with a smaller annual bill impact than a large network upgrade, and the reverse can occur.

Cost causation is a useful principle but not a mechanical formula. If a 1 GW campus requires a dedicated substation, assigning that asset to the campus is relatively straightforward. A new transmission line may also relieve congestion and improve reliability for a broad region. Making the campus pay for the entire line could deter efficient development; making households pay for all of it could subsidize a private project. The regulator needs a defensible estimate of incremental and shared benefits, plus a way to revise obligations if the campus expands or exits. FERC's June 2026 explanation66 separates transmission tariff questions within federal jurisdiction from state decisions about retail charges.

There is another distributional question within the data-center category. A giant operator with a strong balance sheet may provide collateral and self-build some facilities; a smaller firm may rent space from a developer and have little control over its power contract. A tariff designed around the giant could impose impossible terms on the smaller firm, while one designed around the smallest entrant may leave the utility exposed to a very large cancellation. Rules should scale with the size, specificity, and risk of the requested upgrade. They should also disclose how much capacity remains reserved for each project stage so an established customer cannot hoard a scarce connection indefinitely.

The bill debate is not settled by finding a correlation between data-center growth and rising regional rates. Fast-growing regions may also be replacing aging assets, rebuilding after storms, and facing higher fuel or labor costs. A serious empirical study needs matched regions, rate-case records, timing of investments, and attention to selection: data centers may choose places where electricity was cheap or reliable to begin with. No such study can perfectly observe the world without data centers. That is why transparent project-level cost allocation and public review matter more than a national claim that one industry explains every price increase.

15. The stranded-upgrade problem#

Suppose a utility builds a $500 million substation and line for a proposed 1 GW campus. If the campus uses the full connection for decades, its tariffs and dedicated payments may recover the cost. If it uses 200 MW for five years and leaves, regulators must decide who carries the unrecovered balance. A simple annual revenue calculation is insufficient because depreciation, allowed return, taxes, operating cost, peak contribution, and the allocation of shared upgrades determine the bill. The model in the accompanying research package gives a deliberately small sensitivity example, not a tariff estimate.

FERC's June 2026 large-load proceedings67 asked regional grid operators to justify or change connection rules. FERC's cost-transparency explanation68 separates federal transmission tariffs from state retail-rate authority and discusses cost-recovery agreements if a project cancels or departs early. Those proceedings were not a universal final rule as of this draft's cutoff. A defensible tariff can require financial security, milestone payments, minimum demand commitments, and a fair allocation of shared benefits, with the details tested in public proceedings.

The stranded-upgrade problem begins before a customer cancels. A utility may need to order a transformer before the final customer contract is signed because the factory lead time exceeds the construction schedule. If it waits, the project misses its promised date. If it orders, a changed AI market or a failed financing round can leave a specialized asset unused. The project developer may have paid a deposit, but whether that deposit covers the utility's full exposure depends on equipment resale value, shared network benefits, financing costs, and the terms of the tariff. A promise that 'the data center pays' is incomplete until the contract says what happens when the load arrives late, draws less, or leaves.

Several instruments can divide this risk. A reservation fee prices scarce capacity while a project is still optional. Construction milestones release unused reservations. A minimum monthly demand charge can recover an upgrade over time even if actual energy use is low. An exit fee can cover remaining dedicated costs if a customer departs early. Collateral or a parent guarantee can make those promises credible. Phased service lets a campus start at 100 MW while the next substation is built, reducing both the customer's delay and the utility's overbuild risk. The IEA's October 2026 policy survey69 documents versions of these approaches across jurisdictions. None is universally fair; each needs a clear baseline and an appeal process.

Flexibility changes the economics further. A customer willing to curtail during a limited number of critical hours may be able to connect sooner or avoid some capacity investment. But 'willing' has to be a contractual obligation with measurable performance, notice, penalties, and a plan for the customer's backup operation. Otherwise the utility may count a peak reduction that disappears in an emergency. A non-firm connection might also create value for the customer if time to service is worth more than a few interruptions. The IEA estimates large potential global hosting capacity from non-firm arrangements, but that model is not a promise that any particular U.S. node can accept another gigawatt. Local contingency studies decide.

The moral hazard runs both ways. If a developer bears none of the risk of overstatement, it may reserve too much. If the utility can recover every speculative investment from captive households, it may build too eagerly. If a regulator refuses all anticipatory construction, genuinely valuable projects may go elsewhere or wait years. A sound rule places material early-stage risk on the party that controls the project information, permits recovery for upgrades with broad public benefits, and requires evidence at each milestone. It should be revisited as experience accumulates. The first wave of AI campuses is providing data on realization that the existing tariff system did not have when many rules were written.

One can make the issue concrete without pretending to know a statewide bill effect. Suppose a $500 million upgrade has an illustrative 8% annual capital recovery factor. A rough annualized capital payment is $40 million before operating costs, taxes, and other tariff details. At full 1 GW use with an 80% load factor, annual energy would be about 7.0 TWh; the capital payment alone averages about 0.57 cents per kWh. At 200 MW use with the same load factor, the same fixed payment spread over five times less energy averages about 2.85 cents. This is a sensitivity calculation, not a utility rate. It reveals why a project's utilization and minimum commitments are as important as the headline construction cost. The exact formula and assumptions are in the accompanying technical appendix.

The arithmetic does not by itself say who pays the difference. Suppose a contract recovered $40 million through a fixed annual charge backed by collateral. Then the customer's low energy use would not automatically shift that capital payment to households, although the customer's own effective cents per kWh would rise. Suppose instead the contract recovered $40 million only by charging a volume-based rate calibrated to 1 GW at 80% use. If the campus operated at 200 MW, the same charge per kWh would collect only $8 million, leaving a $32 million annual gap before any other terms. Whether that gap stays with shareholders, is covered by a guarantee, is recovered from other customers, or is offset by another user's benefit is a tariff and contract question. The illustrative rate figure visualizes this exposure, not an actual pass-through. A statement that a data center 'pays its own way' should identify its minimum payment, security and exit obligation.

There is an opposite accounting risk. A new transmission line may be needed for a campus's first phase but also relieve a constraint that already costs other customers money and improve service in future years. Charging the campus every dollar would assign the shared benefit to others for free. The defensible comparison is a no-campus plan and a campus plan under the same reliability criterion, then an allocation of the incremental cost and identifiable shared benefits. That calculation changes if the campus is delayed, uses onsite generation, or curtails at the network's tight hours. It must be revisited with actual construction bids and forecast vintages; a national average household bill has none of this information. The methods companion provides the capital-recovery formula and a small transmission example precisely so the variables remain visible.

The rate-policy landscape is changing faster than a settled textbook can describe. The IEA's October 2026 survey70 counts 134 policies and regulations addressing data-center integration across 34 jurisdictions, nearly 40% announced or implemented within the preceding year. Most seek better visibility into how much load will arrive, when, and where. Fewer address the electrical behavior of that load once connected. This order of attention is understandable: a planner cannot study a project it cannot locate. It also means that tariffs written for ordinary industrial demand may need a second round of work on fault ride-through, ramp rates, and verifiable curtailment. A new policy count is evidence of institutional response, not evidence that the rules are already effective.

Corporate energy procurement is changing as well. The same IEA survey records data-center operators signing 45 TWh of power-purchase agreements in the first half of 2026, nearly 70% of that volume in the United States. Those are contracted annual volumes, not 45 TWh of generation that had already reached customers in six months. A contract can help finance new supply and hedge future prices. Its reliability value depends on commissioning, location, hourly output, transmission rights, and what backs the customer when the contracted resource is unavailable. A ratepayer analysis should credit real additions and cost commitments while resisting the temptation to equate a PPA headline with a new firm plant at the customer's node.

16. Communities are part of the circuit#

Transmission corridors and data centers occupy land. Cooling systems consume water in some designs and climates. Local air quality may be affected by onsite engines or backup generators. Construction brings jobs and tax receipts, but permanent employment can be modest relative to electrical demand. These are not side issues to be settled after a technical plan. They determine whether a project can be sited, whether its costs are accepted, and whether its claimed benefits accrue to the people living nearby.

The economics are also not a zero-sum ranking of 'worthy' customers. A semiconductor fab, an AI campus, new housing, and a hospital can all have value. The grid still needs a transparent rule for scarce connection capacity and a way to price the upgrades each imposes. When local approval becomes a bottleneck, it is often because the benefits, costs, and risks have been presented at different levels of detail. Better siting asks for measured water and emissions plans, enforceable cost commitments, and a realistic load ramp, then compares the proposal with alternatives in that location.

Water illustrates why an energy-only account is incomplete. Cooling technology, climate, water source, and operating temperature determine a facility's direct withdrawals and consumption. Evaporative cooling can save electricity but consume water; dry cooling can save water while increasing energy use. The electricity supplying the campus may also require water at power plants. The IEA's October 2026 detailed analysis71 places 34% of identified AI-dedicated data-center capacity in areas with high or extremely high baseline water stress as of September 2026 and emphasizes the water-energy tradeoff. The report's executive summary rounds this to “nearly 40%.” The underlying dataset does not identify cooling technology at each site, so the figure is a geographic risk indicator, not a measure of water consumed or local supplies exhausted.

A good local proposal should provide expected water withdrawals and consumption by season, cooling design, drought contingency, source of supply, and the effect on other users. It should distinguish potable water from reclaimed water and state whether onsite backup generation will run only in emergencies or routinely. Emissions estimates should include those generators and any new grid supply induced by the load, with uncertainty about dispatch. A blanket rule based on facility square footage will miss the difference between a highly utilized AI cluster and a low-density storage site. A blanket promise of 'zero water' can simply move consumption upstream to generation. Measurement and public reporting are more useful than branding.

Employment and tax claims need the same discipline. Construction can employ many people for a short period, while steady operation may employ far fewer. A tax incentive may reduce the very public revenue used to justify the project. On the other hand, a campus can support a local supplier ecosystem and anchor generation or transmission investments that benefit others. The local government should compare net fiscal revenue after abatements and infrastructure costs, not gross assessed value, and it should specify whether community benefits are enforceable. Some costs, such as water scarcity or land fragmentation, are not captured in a utility tariff and need a separate public decision.

Texas has begun to ask for broader information. ERCOT's November 2026 community-impact request72 seeks information on large data centers' water use and local effects, with a later report to follow. The November response set is a subset of responding projects, not a new systemwide total comparable with ERCOT's earlier large-load queue snapshots. Treating the difference between these totals as a collapse in demand would repeat the very mistake this essay criticizes. The value of the request is that it starts connecting electrical plans to the physical communities that host the facilities.

People can reasonably disagree about whether a particular site is worth its costs. A fair process makes that disagreement legible: a dated load ramp, a transparent upgrade plan, a water and emissions budget, emergency operating rules, and a clear account of who benefits. The best technical design may still be rejected if the community judges its land or water too valuable for the project. That is a real constraint, not evidence that the grid's physics was wrong. An infrastructure strategy that assumes consent without earning it will repeatedly mistake proposals for projects.

VI. The AI race becomes an energy race#

17. A GPU order is not a powered cluster#

Useful AI compute requires more than accelerators. A frontier campus needs a site, grid connection or onsite generation, step-down transformers, switchgear, uninterrupted power, backup fuel, cooling, network equipment, and commissioning. Its electrical load may change quickly when jobs start, stop, or when protection systems react to a fault. NERC's May 2026 Level 3 alert73 called for better models, studies, commissioning, fault recording, and direct operational communication with computational loads. The subsequent standards reached Board adoption and a FERC filing by November 2026, but remained subject to regulatory approval. The concern is not just how much energy the servers use but how their electrical behavior interacts with the grid.

Time to power may therefore matter as much as the cents per kilowatt-hour in an AI siting decision. A campus that is ready electrically years before a cheaper site can produce valuable compute earlier. But a dedicated onsite gas plant is not automatically a shortcut: turbines, pipelines, air permits, reliability reserves, and fuel contracts have their own lead times. The IEA's 2026 assessment74 estimates that reliable onsite gas generation may need 30–70% overbuilding relative to load and that only a minority of the tracked pipeline had begun land clearing or construction. A behind-the-meter plant also remains tied to gas and environmental systems even if it is physically separated from the electric grid.

An AI campus's promise to be flexible needs to be translated into the workflow it hosts. A training job may tolerate a scheduled pause or shift in start time, but interruption can waste computation if checkpointing is infrequent. Inference serving a live product may have tight latency and availability commitments. Batch analytics, model evaluation, and preprocessing may be more movable. A colocation operator may own the building but not control its tenants' jobs, which limits the flexibility it can sell. The IEA's October 2026 report75 notes that actively managed compute load remains rare and that the value of AI capacity can make curtailment expensive. A grid tariff should therefore buy a specific, tested response from a specific party, not assume every GPU can be turned off at will.

There is a promising middle ground between unconditional firm service and total isolation. A campus could begin with a non-firm grid connection whose maximum draw varies under published network conditions. It could use batteries for short disturbances and shift selected batch jobs during a constrained hour. As transmission and generation arrive, the service could ramp toward firm capacity. This design can turn time to power into a negotiated tradeoff. It is only credible if the operator can observe the campus load, give notice, verify response, and enforce limits. The campus also needs a realistic financial model for interrupted compute. The IEA's global estimate76 of 750–900 GW of potential extra hosting from non-firm connections is a scenario about network utilization, not a forecast of U.S. data-center deployment.

Power procurement raises a second confusion. A power-purchase agreement can finance a renewable plant and match a company's annual consumption on paper, while the data center still draws from the local grid at hours when that plant produces little. Better matching can consider the hour and region, but even an hourly contract is not a physical dedicated wire unless it includes delivery and balancing. A firm clean-energy claim needs to describe what supplies the load during difficult hours, how storage recharges, and what backup it uses. Conversely, a grid-supplied campus is not automatically a net burden if it finances additional generation and network upgrades and takes curtailment when needed. The claim should follow the actual dispatch and contracts.

The AI energy constraint is also an opportunity to improve computation itself. More efficient chips, model architectures, serving systems, and workload placement can lower joules per useful task. The phrase 'useful task' matters: performance must be comparable, and a faster chip that invites a larger workload may not reduce total electricity use. Scheduling batch work in a region and hour with abundant supply can reduce marginal system strain, though data movement, latency, and hardware utilization impose costs. Cooling design can improve facility efficiency but may trade energy against water. These are engineering choices with real promise. They should be evaluated against measured workload energy, not by announcing that an abstract algorithm will solve a physical bottleneck.

Finally, speed can make waste rational for an individual firm while remaining costly for everyone else. A company may pay a premium for earlier electricity because the option to train or serve a frontier model sooner is valuable. If its contract leaves shared grid costs with households, the social cost exceeds the private price. If it pays for its connection and accepts curtailment, its urgency can finance capacity that benefits others. The design of the connection agreement therefore becomes part of the AI industrial strategy. It determines whether scarce power is allocated to credible high-value work and whether the public is compensated for infrastructure built in anticipation of it.

18. The comparison with China is not one number#

The United States and China have different electricity systems, industrial structures, siting rules, and data transparency. The IEA's 2026 tables77 estimate U.S. data-center electricity use at 224 TWh in 2025 and 426 TWh in its 2030 base case, versus 117 and 277 TWh for China. Those are modeled electricity quantities, not a scoreboard for useful AI output. Chips, algorithms, network quality, software, capital, and utilization all mediate the conversion from kilowatt-hours to capability.

Still, electricity infrastructure is strategic. A country that can add generation but cannot deliver it to compute and factories may lose time. A country that builds quickly but relies on a concentrated fuel or equipment supply chain inherits another vulnerability. The right comparison would track time from request to energized service, delivered cost and reliability, transformer and turbine supply, new transmission, emissions, and useful compute per unit of energy. Cross-country charts that compare only annual generation will miss the relevant bottleneck.

The geopolitical comparison is often framed as a race between American innovation and Chinese construction. That framing captures a real tension but conceals critical variables. The United States may have more frontier chips and software talent while facing fragmented siting and lengthy grid connections. China may build power and transmission at a different scale and speed while facing its own regional congestion, technology restrictions, fuel mix, and opaque demand data. Neither country's annual electricity output is a direct measure of AI capability. A useful comparison is the time and cost to deliver reliable power to a computing campus, adjusted for the compute that power can actually support.

The IEA's 2030 U.S. and China data-center estimates are model outputs, and the gap between them is not a forecast of who wins an AI race. One TWh consumed on older or underutilized hardware can produce less useful work than the same energy on a modern cluster. Model architecture, software optimization, network throughput, memory, and the availability of leading accelerators affect performance. Electricity is a necessary input, not a sufficient one. Conversely, extraordinary chips sitting in boxes behind an unfinished substation produce no frontier training. The race is a chain of complements; a weakness at any link can determine the realized output.

Supply chains make the comparison more complex. Transformers, turbines, switchgear, high-voltage cable, semiconductors, and cooling equipment cross borders. A country can have abundant primary energy and still wait for components. Export controls can constrain chips while trade disruptions constrain electrical hardware. Large grid projects can be delayed by land rights and coordination among jurisdictions. Resilience requires diversification, domestic capability where economically justified, and inventories for components whose replacement time is strategically unacceptable. It does not follow that every component must be manufactured domestically; that would raise costs and could create concentration in a different form.

The United States also has a geographic advantage that national charts miss: multiple regions with different climates, resource mixes, and industrial histories. Some may have generation and land but limited transmission; others have demand, capital, and an expensive local network. Moving a latency-tolerant workload between regions can be cheaper than moving a gigawatt through a new line, but only if data, latency, security, and customer contracts allow it. The choice is not simply to build more generation or lose the race. It is to place compute where reliable power can be delivered soonest, while building longer-lived networks for broader economic use.

One potential upside is underappreciated. The same AI boom that stresses grids can fund new generation, equipment factories, control software, and skilled labor. The IEA's September 2026 grid report78 estimates that better use of existing networks could unlock additional connections globally and argues for better measurements, digital tools, and operator training. AI may improve forecasting or asset inspection, but safety-critical control still requires validation, auditability, and accountable operators. The practical opportunity is not an autonomous AI running the grid. It is a larger market for the slow physical and institutional improvements the grid already needed.

The geopolitical test, then, should be expressed as a dashboard rather than a scoreboard. Track actual energized data-center megawatts, time from credible application to firm service, delivered electricity cost, interruption performance, useful compute per MWh, incremental emissions and water use, and the fraction of upgrades paid by the beneficiary. Track these by region and over time. A country that excels at one metric while failing another may still be competitive, but the dashboard would reveal the tradeoff. It would also prevent a large announced project pipeline from masquerading as deployed capacity.

VII. The next ten years#

19. Three futures, none of them predictions#

The accompanying model poses a controlled question: what if incremental U.S. large-load power reaching operation in 2030 were 10, 30, or 60 GW, each at an 80% average load factor? They would use about 70, 210, or 420 TWh a year. By 2035, illustrative operating loads of 20, 60, or 120 GW would use about 140, 420, or 841 TWh. These numbers are arithmetic sensitivities, not forecasts; they deliberately avoid assigning a probability or treating a connection request as a realized project. They also do not claim that this load is entirely additional to Berkeley Lab's data-center estimates.

The low case still requires local delivery and reliable operation. The middle case tests whether generation and transmission can expand together. The high case would be a major national build challenge and could prove internally inconsistent if equipment, capital, or AI demand fails to support it. The model should be extended with regional hourly profiles, accredited capacity, fuel, transmission limits, and actual project-stage realization before anyone uses it to make an adequacy claim. Its most useful output today is the scale of the unit conversion and the questions it forces a planner to ask.

The scenario arithmetic is deliberately spare because false precision is the enemy here. At 80% utilization, each operating GW consumes 7.008 TWh per year. That identity turns 10, 30, and 60 GW into the 70, 210, and 420 TWh 2030 examples. It does not say what the system peak will be. A 60 GW fleet could have a high coincident contribution if nearly all campuses run flat through summer afternoons; it could have a lower contribution if contracts shift work and require curtailment. Regional concentration changes everything: 10 GW dispersed across the country is not the same transmission problem as 10 GW added around one metropolitan area.

In a low-realization 2030, many speculative campuses fail to finance, delay procurement, or connect in phases. The resulting grid problem is still significant where a few large clusters land. The policy risk is building too much dedicated infrastructure for the abandoned requests and then charging other customers. Successful governance in this world would show high utilization of completed upgrades, quick release of unused reservations, and clear cost recovery from the projects that caused bespoke investment. It would also preserve the ability to expand if demand returns. A low scenario is not automatically a cheap one if planners ordered the wrong equipment early.

In the middle case, tens of operating gigawatts require a sustained build of generation, storage, transmission, and substations. The challenge is synchronization. A plant may be completed before its line; a line before a transformer; a substation before the customer installs servers. Each mismatch ties up capital. Good staging and contractual milestones can reduce this idle time. Resource adequacy would need regional hourly modeling, including weather and fuel. A national average energy addition of 210 TWh is roughly 5% of the 2026 generation forecast, but that percentage says little about the most constrained hours or nodes. The middle scenario tests whether institutions can coordinate schedules, not simply whether enough dollars are available.

The high case is a stress test of both economics and physics. Sixty operating GW by 2030, at 80% load factor, would use about 420 TWh annually, on the order of one tenth of current U.S. generation. It would require not only customers willing to pay for that electricity but sites, chips, transmission, transformers, cooling, fuel or clean supply, and trained operators. If those conditions fail, actual power use will be lower and the scarcity will appear as delayed or displaced compute. If they succeed but costs are allocated poorly, household and industrial customers can experience a burden even while national GDP rises. The model cannot decide which outcome is likely. It can identify the assumptions that would have to hold together.

By 2035, uncertainty compounds. AI efficiency could cut energy per task while demand for tasks multiplies. Industrial and transportation loads may rise independently. Power plants can retire, new lines can arrive, and the risk hour may shift from a summer afternoon to a winter morning in some regions. The illustrative 20/60/120 GW operating cases are best read as boundary conditions for robust decisions. A line with broad benefits may be worth building across them; a highly specialized asset may need a customer guarantee. A policy that makes a connection queue credible helps under all three. The model's scenario output is not a substitute for region-specific integrated resource planning.

The most revealing thing to observe between now and 2030 is not a revised national forecast alone. It is whether the conversion ratios improve. Of requested large-load capacity, how much reaches approved studies, construction, energization, and sustained use? Of queued generation, how much reaches commercial operation and contributes during risk hours? Of planned transmission, how much is permitted and energized? If each stage becomes visible, surprises will be smaller and forecasts can be corrected before billions are spent. If the stages remain opaque, every new headline will tempt us to confuse intention with delivery.

20. What would count as progress#

The first practical improvement is better evidence. Publish large-load requests by stage and historical realization, with confidential details protected. Measure actual energized load against forecast vintages. Report grid-connection lead time, delivered power, and upgrade cost. Separate firm and interruptible service. Pair demand forecasts with credible supply and network milestones. When a plan says a project will be powered in 2029, identify the transformer, substation, fuel, and contingency path that make that statement true.

The accompanying American Grid Stress Atlas is a first attempt at that ledger across seven operator footprints. It keeps each datum's vintage and class visible: PJM's auction procurement gap, ERCOT's request pipeline, CAISO's 2026 modeled surplus, MISO's seasonal auction result and member plans, SPP's existing-only and anticipated-resource cases, plus the summer and winter forecasts in NYISO's 2026 Gold Book and ISO New England's 2026 CELT workbook. The New York and New England tables are now extracted, but their weather, loss and behind-the-meter conventions still differ. Other empty cells show exactly where public material has not yet been transformed into a defensible common comparison. The atlas applies the same hypothetical 1 GW firm and flexible campus to each region as a set of questions, not a claim that any site is ready. A utility connection study, a capacity plan and a contract would have to answer those questions for a real project.

This is also a way to test policy progress. If a new large-load process succeeds, more requests should move through studies and financial commitment into measured operation, while forecasts become better calibrated and upgrade costs remain traceable. If a state adopts a rule that lets a data center connect sooner under curtailment, publish how often the curtailment is called and whether it works during stress. If a transmission plan claims broad benefits, report whether the line finishes, what congestion or reliability constraint it relieved, and who paid. The public does not need to know a sensitive substation's defenses to see the aggregate funnel. Better measurements will not remove scarcity, but they can stop a pipeline of options from masquerading as delivered power.

A stage-based ledger should be simple enough to audit. Every regional operator or utility could publish quarterly totals for initial requests, validated site control, completed impact studies, signed cost agreements, construction starts, approved energization, and measured peak and annual use. It should preserve forecast vintages rather than overwrite old ones, so an outside reader can see whether a revised projection improved calibration. Projects can be anonymized where security or competition requires it; aggregate cohorts can still reveal attrition. The same approach belongs on the supply side: generator queues should show which projects have permits, equipment, financing, and a commissioning date. A forecast without a track record of its errors asks the public to trust a curve it cannot inspect.

The second is to build and use more of the system. Complete viable generation and storage; reform interconnection studies; plan transmission over longer horizons; expand transformer and other equipment manufacturing; use existing lines more effectively where engineering evidence supports it; and design flexible-load contracts whose curtailments can be tested. FERC's Order 1920 framework79 requires long-term regional planning and benefit-based cost allocation processes, but an order is a planning framework, not a finished line. The final test is physical: how many dependable megawatts can reach customers at the right hour, and at what cost?

Building more also requires deciding what to build first. A useful ranking considers the probability that a project will be ready, its contribution during local risk hours, its effect on congestion, its permit and equipment schedule, and how many customers benefit. A low-cost solar project with a stalled interconnection may do less for a 2028 winter risk than an existing plant's fuel assurance. A high-cost local battery may relieve a transmission constraint faster than a long line, but only for its discharge duration. A line may have slower completion and much larger benefits over decades. The answer changes by place and year. That is why a national technology quota cannot replace regional engineering and public cost review.

Third, make flexibility a real product. A large load should be able to buy defined non-firm service with clear operating limits and a path to firmer service as capacity is built. Its curtailment capability must be tested under realistic conditions, including what onsite backup does to grid flows. Utilities should value verified peak reductions and not count a promise that is unenforceable. A household should not subsidize a corporate connection whose cheap rate depends on flexibility that disappears during stress. In the other direction, a company that can shift work and accepts a credible service limit should not be forced to wait for a network designed for its theoretical maximum at every hour.

Fourth, turn security and recovery into measured performance. Track exercises, backup control capability, spare equipment compatibility, fuel assurance, restoration curves, and lessons from real disturbances. Distinguish a bulk-system security event from a distribution outage and a data-center fault ride-through failure. Do not publish the exact location or defensive detail of critical facilities. The public can still judge whether resilience is improving through aggregate indicators and independent review. An increase in reported near misses may even be a sign of better detection rather than worsening physics; the metric needs context.

Fifth, defend the ratepayer against both kinds of mistake. Require a large prospective customer to put money at risk as its project matures, and give it a transparent way to earn back or apply that money as it builds. Assign dedicated upgrades to the beneficiary, then allocate broader network benefits using evidence. Make exit obligations and unused-capacity release explicit. At the same time, permit anticipatory investments that serve many plausible futures rather than forcing a utility to wait until every customer is physically on site. The objective is neither zero risk nor perfect prediction. It is an allocation of uncertainty to the parties best able to control it.

Sixth, treat communities as decision-makers. Publish site-level energy and water plans, local emissions, tax terms, and a realistic jobs estimate before public approval. Compare alternative sites and cooling approaches. Offer a way to verify performance after opening, because a proposal can change as tenants and hardware change. A project that promises to bring its own power should disclose what it will do when that plant is down and what grid service it still seeks. Fast construction that evades these questions is likely to create slower conflict later.

Seventh, maintain an honest national view. EIA can report energy trends; NERC can test reliability risk; FERC can address interstate transmission tariffs and markets; states can regulate retail costs and siting; grid operators can publish stage data. The institutions need not become one agency, but their measurements should be compatible. A shared vocabulary for peak, energy, accredited capacity, interconnection request, energization, and realized load would make it harder for a speculative number to migrate from one chart into a claim it cannot support. That is a modest reform with potentially large value in a period of fast-moving investment.

Progress by 2030 would be visible in outcomes, not only in announcements: shorter time from credible application to service, more completed generation and transmission, smaller errors in load forecasts, adequate reserves in stressed hours, fewer costly customer interruptions, and rates that recover new infrastructure fairly. Failure would be visible too. A region might approve enormous request totals while little energizes, or connect load faster by silently socializing upgrades, or preserve nominal reliability while household bills and local water use rise sharply. These are separate dimensions of success. A country can improve one and still owe work on the others.

Return to that ordinary Tuesday. The operator holds frequency, a factory signs a supply agreement, a campus installs its first rack, and most people switch on a light without thinking about any of them. Whether the decade succeeds will be decided by the quiet connections among those events. Did the campus's requested megawatt become a measured one? Did a resource arrive when its capacity auction counted it? Did the transformer ordered years earlier match the substation that needed it? Did the community understand the water and cost commitments before construction, and could it verify them afterward?

The grid can ration time before it rations electricity. A delayed connection can strand chips, a delayed line can strand generation, and a mistaken forecast can strand a bill with customers who never asked for the project. These losses rarely look like a national blackout. They accumulate as years of slower building, higher costs, and narrower choices. The country has room to avoid them. It can measure the real pipeline, price the risk of cancellation, build resources for the hours that matter, connect flexible loads on honest terms, and make recovery a practiced skill. The limit is physical, but it is also institutional. The power that counts is the power that reaches a person or machine at the promised time, at a cost its beneficiaries will bear.
