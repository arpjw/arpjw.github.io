The Truth Behind AI Doomerism

What AI is already costing us, what it could cost us next, and who gets to decide whether it’s worth it.

The bottle of water was an estimate.

Researchers wanted to know how much water an AI conversation might consume. They had no meter attached to the servers. They estimated how much electricity the model used, how much water a data center might consume to cool its equipment, and how much more might be consumed generating its electricity. Under their assumptions, GPT-3, the model family behind the original ChatGPT, could consume half a liter for roughly 10 to 50 medium-length responses. Two years later, Google reported a figure of about five drops for its median Gemini Apps text prompt. Both numbers came from papers. By the time they reached the public, they sounded like rival readings from the same meter.1

A similar thing happened in a high-school math experiment. Students with a chatbot did better on their practice work. Then the researchers took the chatbot away, and the students who had used the unrestricted version did worse on their own. You could choose either result and make a plausible headline about the future of education. The order in which the results occurred was the most important part of the experiment.2

I build AI systems, and I want them to get better. In The Epistemic Century, I argued that cheaper machine intelligence could help institutions do more of the work they routinely skip, like inspecting the evidence behind a claim or checking whether a result holds up. There’s a premise underneath that argument worth revisiting. If AI makes it cheaper to produce an answer, someone still has to decide whether the answer is any good. In a healthy institution, some of the savings go toward doing that job well. In an unhealthy one, the savings go to whoever deploys the system, and the checking falls to whoever gets hurt when it’s wrong.

The word doomerism covers remarkably different beliefs. The homeowner worried about a substation, the illustrator worried about her next commission, and the researcher worried about losing control of a future AI system are making claims about different events with different evidence. Lumping them together suits anyone who wants a quick verdict. It gives the enthusiastic builder permission to dismiss a concern about water by arguing about extinction. It gives the critic permission to cite a documented harm and carry that certainty into predictions the evidence can barely touch.

I want to know what survives when each claim has to stand on its own. Start with a place where someone has to put a dollar figure on the answer.

The town that pays for the cloud#

In December 2024, Virginia’s legislative watchdog published a study of the state’s data center industry. It found that existing electricity rates appropriately allocated the costs utilities were then incurring, including the costs of serving data centers. It also projected that, as demand rose, a typical Dominion residential customer could face an additional $14 to $37 a month in generation and transmission costs by 2040, in inflation-adjusted dollars. The second finding sounds like an accusation against the first. Read the study in sequence and you can see the problem the legislature was trying to solve.3

Serving a customer on the grid you already have is one transaction. Building a grid for the customer you expect to have fifteen years from now is another. A utility may have to plan transmission lines, substations, and generation years before a large site runs at full capacity. If demand comes in on schedule, the new customer buys a great deal of power and pays charges over a long period. If the project arrives late, is smaller than expected, or is canceled, some of the spending may already have happened. And even when a large facility uses everything it asked for, the extra demand can make the next unit of power more expensive for everyone served by the same system. An honest bill for one customer can coexist with higher bills for its neighbors.4

Virginia’s response was to change the terms of the transaction. In November 2025, the State Corporation Commission approved Dominion’s GS-5 rate class for qualifying large customers, scheduled to take effect in January 2027. For customers subject to the relevant minimums, distribution and transmission demand charges are based on at least 85% of contracted demand, and generation demand charges on at least 60%. New large-load contracts covered by the rule carry a minimum 14-year obligation, with collateral requirements for customers without sufficient credit. Eligibility, exemptions, and exactly which charges apply matter in a real bill. The point of the order is visible even in a stripped-down example.5

Suppose a facility subject to both minimums contracts for 100 megawatts and records a monthly billing demand of 50 megawatts before the floors apply. The distribution and transmission components would each be billed on at least 85 MW. The generation component would be billed on at least 60 MW. Its energy use would still be counted separately in megawatt-hours. The 85 and the 60 are bases for different charges. You can see what the rule accomplishes without inventing a household savings figure. A thinner month for the data center leaves it with more of the bill for the capacity it asked the utility to reserve.6

There’s still plenty the rule leaves to decide. Which shared upgrades are attributable to large customers? How much of a long-lived investment is recovered over the contract term? How do other customers fare if the whole region must buy more power during expensive hours? Virginia’s commission directed Dominion to propose changes to how it allocates certain shared costs. That is why I would leave the 2024 household estimate in the story and date it prominently. It tells you what the state feared under the assumptions of that study. The subsequent order tells you what regulators tried to change. A household bill after the reform would require a new estimate.7

Loudoun County also has a persuasive reason to want the industry. The county says data centers generated $1.2 billion in real and personal property tax revenue in fiscal year 2026. State researchers found that much of the economic activity comes during construction. Industry representatives told them a typical 250,000-square-foot building might have around 50 full-time workers, roughly half of them contractors. Put those figures side by side and you can understand why a county budget officer, an electrician, and a resident across the road might have three different views of the same facility. They’re looking at different parts of the deal.8

The global numbers tell you how often that deal may have to be negotiated. The International Energy Agency’s April 2026 update put electricity consumption by all data centers at about 485 TWh in 2025. Its central projection reaches 950 TWh in 2030, around 3% of global electricity demand. AI-focused facilities account for a faster-growing part of that total. Three percent is a meaningful share of world electricity. It also gives little guidance to the utility that has to serve several large campuses in the same corridor, on the same schedule. The global figure measures energy over a year. A utility has to deliver power at the hours it’s needed, on lines that reach a particular place.9

The emissions question has the same geography. Ask which electricity a new site draws during the hours it runs and what gets built to keep supplying it. An annual purchase agreement can finance new renewable generation, which matters. The annual sum alone cannot tell you what supplies an additional load on a still evening. On-site gas can help a project bridge a grid bottleneck while moving some emissions and pollution closer to the project. An efficient rack can lower the energy cost of one task while making it cheap enough to run many more tasks. Each claim describes a different part of the outcome, and an operator’s favorite metric will usually describe the part it manages best.10

Back to the bottle#

The original bottle estimate included water consumed on site and water associated with electricity generation. It used assumptions about the electricity GPT-3 needed to answer a request and allowed cooling demand to vary with conditions. Google’s figure used production data to estimate the energy of its median Gemini Apps text prompt in May 2025, including equipment and facility overhead. It then used a fleet-level water-efficiency factor to estimate direct freshwater consumption for serving that prompt. Google had better access to its own equipment. The earlier study drew a wider boundary around the water. The results answer different questions before you even get to the different systems and years.11

Water withdrawal measures how much is taken from a source. Water consumption measures the portion that is unavailable for reuse in the same local system over the relevant period, often because it evaporated. Both can matter. A facility can return much of what it withdraws and still put pressure on a water system during a dry spell. Another facility may consume little water at the property line but rely on electricity whose generation consumes water elsewhere. Chip fabrication adds another boundary if you’re estimating the life-cycle footprint. A useful disclosure tells you which source is affected, on what schedule, for what activity, with what alternatives.12

The IEA reported in October 2026 that nearly 40% of the identified capacity of data centers dedicated to AI was in areas of high or extremely high baseline water stress. That measures where capacity is located, and actual water use depends on how those sites are operated. A dry-cooled facility and one that depends heavily on evaporation can have different local impacts in the same watershed. Dry cooling can require more electricity under some conditions. Reclaimed water can reduce demand on drinking-water supplies, with the value depending on what the reclaimed water would otherwise be used for. These are engineering and planning choices that a national per-prompt average can obscure.13

If I lived next to a proposed campus, I’d want its expected peak electricity demand, its water source and expected summer withdrawals and consumption, the terms under which it could be curtailed, and the contract that says who pays for the upgrades. The number of drops in an average prompt would tell me very little about whether to support the permit.

The homework got better. Did the student?#

A student who can copy the area of a triangle has completed a problem. A student who can explain why two copies of the triangle make a parallelogram can reconstruct the formula next week. A teacher can distinguish the two by asking one more question. A chatbot can erase the distinction by giving the student both answers before they’ve tried either.

Hamsa Bastani and colleagues studied nearly a thousand high-school math students. One group practiced without a chatbot. Another could use a GPT-4 interface that behaved much like a general chatbot. A third had a version designed around tutoring safeguards. During practice, both AI groups did better than the control group. On a later assessment without the tool, the unrestricted group scored 17% lower relative to the control group. The version designed to protect learning largely mitigated the drop. The study measures performance in a specific educational setting, and the important intervention was a change in how students could get help.14

The result shouldn’t surprise anyone who has watched a student get unstuck by looking at the back of a textbook. What’s new is how quickly an assistant can remove every obstacle in the path to a finished assignment. It can do the original calculation, explain the calculation in the language the student is supposed to use, and produce a second explanation when the first sounds too polished. A submission can look like evidence that practice happened even when most of the practice was skipped. That is a problem for the student and for the teacher who uses the assignment to decide what to teach tomorrow.

There’s good evidence that the same interface can be used differently. In a randomized college-physics study, Greg Kestin and colleagues found that students using a purpose-built AI tutor learned more in less time than students in the in-class active-learning condition studied. The team designed the tutor around particular lessons and teaching practices. Put that alongside the math experiment and the useful question becomes surprisingly practical. What does the tool ask the student to do before supplying the next step? When the student makes an error, does the tutor diagnose it, replace the answer, or move on? What can the student still do when the tool is gone?15

The evidence gets thinner as the claims get larger. A much-discussed essay-writing preprint used EEG in an experiment with 54 participants in the first three sessions and 18 in a fourth. It reported differences in brain connectivity and difficulty recalling or quoting work under some conditions. Those are findings about people doing a particular writing task under particular conditions. EEG is a measure of electrical activity, and the experiment did not measure a loss of general intelligence. A separate survey of 319 knowledge workers found that higher confidence in generative AI was associated with less reported critical-thinking effort. Workers also described spending effort on verifying and integrating outputs. A survey like that can tell us what people say happens to their work. To find out what skills they lose or gain, we’d need to test them over time.16

I’m happy to offload the part of a task I already understand and am unlikely to benefit from repeating. I’m more wary when I can’t tell whether I understand the part I’m offloading. If I ask a model to rewrite a sentence I’ve already thought through, I can usually tell when it’s changed the meaning. If I ask it to give me a position on a topic I’ve never studied, a fluent answer may conceal exactly the gaps I would have discovered by trying to write it myself.

There’s an accessibility benefit here that the argument about cheating tends to flatten. Getting an explanation in a different language, hearing a passage read aloud, or asking the same question a third time can make it possible to do the intellectual work in the first place. A student who needed help reading the question may still be fully capable of solving it. The hard part of designing a classroom policy is identifying the skill the assignment is supposed to exercise, then giving students whatever help lets them exercise it.

I’d let an AI tutor into a classroom if it could improve what students can do next week, in the conditions in which they’ll actually need the skill. I’d give it less credit for making today’s assignment easier to grade. A school should know the difference before it signs the contract.

The missing first job#

A firm can automate the work of an apprentice and still need a master. For a while, that looks like a good arrangement. There are plenty of experienced people around. They can review the output and handle the cases the system can’t. The trouble shows up later, when the firm wants to hire someone who knows which cases those are.

That is the version of the jobs argument I take most seriously right now. A lot of entry-level work looks wasteful when judged by the output alone. A junior analyst checks a spreadsheet a senior analyst could check faster. An associate researches a question whose answer the partner probably suspects. A new engineer fixes a small bug in a part of the codebase that somebody else knows by heart. Those tasks produce modest goods for the firm and something valuable for the worker. After enough of them, the worker gets a feel for where mistakes hide and when to raise a hand.

The evidence on productivity gives firms a reason to change that arrangement. In a study of a generative AI tool rolled out to customer-support agents, Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li, and Lindsey Raymond found a 15% increase in successfully resolved chats per hour, with larger benefits for less experienced and lower-skilled workers. Part of what makes the study interesting is that the tool helped a newcomer do the job. One possible response is to give more work to people who can now do it. Another is to cover the same volume with a smaller staff. The productivity estimate alone leaves the hiring decision open.17

Productivity results also depend on the job. In an early-2025 randomized study, METR found that experienced open-source developers took longer on tasks in familiar repositories when allowed to use the AI tools available at the time. These were real tasks for people who knew the code well. The follow-up work describes difficulties measuring the effect as tools and working habits changed. The original study remains evidence about that tool generation and that workflow. I’d want to know how often a coding assistant is working from context the developer already has, how much review it creates, and whether the saved keystrokes survive a count of the total time.18

The labor market adds another layer. Using ADP payroll records, Stanford researchers reported in August 2026 that employment among workers aged 22 to 25 in highly AI-exposed occupations stood about 19% below the level it would have reached had it kept pace with employment among comparably aged workers in less-exposed occupations. The adjustment in their data appeared to come primarily through reduced hiring. The comparison is observational. It is evidence of a divergence that deserves attention, and the share attributable to AI is harder to establish. Industries that differ in exposure also differ in hiring cycles, financial conditions, and who was hired during the previous boom.19

Other data make an economy-wide verdict premature. The Budget Lab at Yale found no clear evidence of broad disruption associated with AI in its analysis through September 2026. In Denmark, Anders Humlum and Emilie Vestergaard documented rapid adoption and changes in how work was organized, alongside no detectable impact on earnings or recorded hours in the period they studied. A slowdown in hiring for some U.S. workers entering exposed occupations can coexist with modest aggregate changes, and a Danish wage record measures something different from an American job candidate’s chance of getting an offer.20

I think the most important evidence over the next few years will be hiring, training, and wages by experience level, traced within firms as they adopt these tools. A task can be exposed long before it is economical to automate. A firm can automate it without dismissing anyone. And the career cost of a hiring freeze can be serious even if most members of the affected cohort eventually find another job. Someone who would have entered a field at 22 can miss several years of learning and advancement that are difficult to recover later.

There’s a reason an individual firm might underinvest in fixing this. It carries the cost of training a junior, while much of the benefit can walk out the door and join a competitor. If firms believe they’ll always be able to hire experienced people elsewhere, each can sensibly train fewer of its own. At industry scale, the result would be a thinner pool of people competent to review work they never had a chance to do themselves. Any business planning to rely on experienced reviewers should be able to say how the next ones will learn.

The internet after proof gets expensive#

Two days before New Hampshire’s 2024 Democratic presidential primary, voters received robocalls with a voice that sounded like Joe Biden’s, telling them to refrain from voting in the primary. The FCC investigated and issued a $6 million forfeiture order against the political consultant behind the campaign. The public record documents an attempt to impersonate a president and suppress participation. It leaves a much harder question about how many people changed their behavior because of the call.21

The call is unsettling even if very few people believed it. All the recipient needed to do was hesitate. A person confronted with a plausible voice has to switch from listening to investigating. Where did the call come from? Is there an official announcement? Is there time to check? The attacker pays once to produce and distribute the impersonation. The recipients pay separately to work out whether it’s real. The same imbalance applies to a fabricated legal citation, a fake review, and an intimate image made without the subject’s consent. Proof becomes more expensive for the person with the least information and, often, the least time.

In Mata v. Avianca, lawyers submitted cases generated by ChatGPT that did not exist. The court’s sanctions order describes how long the episode went on after the authorities were challenged. The useful lesson is in what happened between the chatbot and the court. An invented case is cheap to produce. Establishing that a plausible-looking case is fictitious can require searching several databases, checking the names and docket numbers, and giving the person who submitted it a chance to explain. A lawyer who asks a system for ten cases still owns the cost of checking ten cases. To find out whether the system saved time, count the checking.22

That bill is especially cruel when it falls on someone in an intimate deepfake. Proving the image was fabricated may leave the original harm intact. Other people have already seen it, copies may be circulating, and the person depicted is left dealing with platforms that know far more about where it traveled than they do. In the U.S., the TAKE IT DOWN Act requires covered platforms to provide a process for valid removal requests and to act within 48 hours. That puts a time limit on part of the burden. It also puts real weight on how a platform handles disputed requests, protects the complainant’s privacy, and searches for copies. Those details determine what happens after someone presses the report button.23

Media provenance can help where a source has a reason to sign its work. A newsroom can attach a verifiable record to an image. A government office can publish an announcement through an authenticated channel. A valid credential can establish something about who signed a file and what it says about its history. You still need to know whether the signer had the facts right. And a genuine recording by someone without access to credentialing tools still deserves to be evaluated on its merits. Provenance works best as another piece of evidence a person can consult, alongside corroboration and the track record of the publisher.24

The slower change may matter more than the memorable hoax. When a search engine generates the answer at the top of the page, a user can get real convenience and the source can lose the visit that helps pay for the work. In a study of March 2025 browsing activity, Pew found that users clicked a traditional result on 8% of visits with a Google AI summary, compared with 15% of visits without one. Different queries can attract different kinds of results and different user behavior, so treat that as an observed association. It still points to a business question worth taking seriously. Who will finance the local reporting, original photography, review, or technical documentation that makes the answer worth generating?25

People call the cheap output slop, which is fair enough until the word substitutes for a diagnosis. The problem has as much to do with distribution as with generation. A service that earns money from impressions has a reason to post another story as long as its expected revenue exceeds the trivial cost of making it. A platform can profit from serving the post while readers do the filtering. Better writing models will remove some of the giveaways that make this material easy to spot. They’ll do little for the reader who needs to know who checked the facts and who will correct them tomorrow.

Who gets paid for culture?#

The copyright argument gets easier to follow if you imagine an actual book and what happens to it. Someone acquires a copy. Someone makes another copy for use in training. The resulting system may produce a passage that resembles the book closely, or it may produce a new book that competes for the same readers without resembling any particular passage. A writer can have a serious objection at each stage. The legal analysis can come out differently at each one.

In Bartz v. Anthropic, a U.S. district judge held that the copying at issue for training was fair use. The ruling treated the acquisition and retention of pirated books for a central library separately and rejected fair use for that conduct on the record before the court. The U.S. Copyright Office’s Part 3 report, issued in prepublication form, examines factors including the purpose of the use, the source of the material, and effects on markets for the works. A particular ruling can resolve important facts for particular parties. A developer still has to ask how it obtained its data, what it did with the copies, and what its system makes available to users.26

There’s a legitimate public interest in letting people study culture and build new things out of what they learn. An arrangement that required negotiating individually with every rightsholder before anyone could train a useful model could strongly favor companies with the most money and lawyers. There’s also a legitimate objection when a company industrializes access to creative work, gives the people who made it little say over the terms, and then sells a substitute to their clients. Both arguments become more concrete when the records exist to distinguish a public-domain collection, a licensed archive, a collection acquired from unauthorized sources, and a product that reproduces passages. Without records, builders and creators end up arguing over a process only one side was able to see.

Even a world with clean licensing would have a question about what people make. In a short-fiction experiment, Anil Doshi and Oliver Hauser found that access to AI-generated ideas improved ratings of individual stories, particularly for less creative writers in the study, while making stories more similar to one another. Imagine an editor reading one submission and finding it better than it would have been. Then imagine reading a hundred submissions with ideas supplied by the same system. The editor may value the improvement and still miss the range. The study measured short stories in an experiment. It gives us a reason to look for the same effect elsewhere, including in systems whose users repeatedly ask for the most likely first answer.27

There’s a related fear that AI-generated material will fill the web and models will then get worse by training on it. Recursive-training experiments show that degradation can occur when models repeatedly learn from generated samples, especially when those samples replace the original data. Other experiments found that accumulating generated data alongside real data could avoid collapse in the settings studied. The relevant questions are whether the new data is replacing earlier evidence, whether anyone can check it, and whether the rare cases are still represented. A synthetic math problem with a verifiable answer and a synthetic product review presented as a real purchase are both generated text. One can teach a system something by construction. The other claims an observation that never happened.28

I worry less about whether a machine can make something I’d call art than about whether people can keep making work that takes time and gives them a reason to look closely at the world. A model can produce ten thousand plausible descriptions of a town council meeting. The reporter who sat through it can tell you what the council did after most of the audience went home. If the economics work for the first activity and fail for the second, a very capable system will have less of substance to summarize.

Who can overrule the machine?#

A chatbot making up a case is easy to recognize once someone checks. A system can do something more consequential while executing its assignment perfectly.

In a widely used health-care algorithm studied by Ziad Obermeyer and colleagues, the system used expected health spending as a proxy for medical need. The researchers found Black patients were considerably sicker than white patients at the same risk score. Health spending reflected, among other things, unequal access to care. A model could predict spending accurately and still systematically understate who needed help. The relevant choice was the target. A bigger model trained to predict the same target could have performed better on the vendor’s metric and preserved the underlying problem.29

That study predates the current wave of generative AI. It belongs here because a conversational interface can give an old institutional error a more convincing explanation. A hiring tool can generate a reason that sounds like the product of deliberation. A screening tool can describe a decision with confidence even when the category came from a crude proxy. The person affected needs to know what evidence was used and how to challenge it. A polished explanation is of little use if the person who issued it has no authority to reverse the decision.

The FTC’s case against Rite Aid illustrates what can happen when a false positive reaches an employee with permission to act. The agency alleged that the retailer deployed facial recognition without reasonable safeguards, leading to false matches and harmful encounters with shoppers. The institutional failure involved what staff were told to do, how the company monitored errors, and what remedy was available to a person misidentified in a store. An employee seeing an alert may technically be a human in the loop. Whether they can evaluate the evidence and disregard the alert is the question that matters.30

Privacy requires the same attention to the people and the workflow. An assistant may receive information a user never intended to share with a vendor, such as a patient’s history in a pasted note or a colleague’s salary in an attached spreadsheet. That raises questions about retention, internal access, and downstream use. In a workplace, the same tool that helps a team search its documents can give an employer a finer-grained record of what workers say and do. I would want to know which of those capabilities were necessary for the stated job and whether employees or customers could use the service without consenting to the rest.31

Companion chatbots make the incentive problem unusually personal. A four-week controlled study involving 981 participants found no significant effects across its randomly assigned interaction modes and conversation types on the psychosocial outcomes it measured. Participants who voluntarily used the chatbot more had worse outcomes on several measures. The heavier use was self-selected, so the result leaves open whether use contributed to the outcomes, whether people already experiencing difficulties used it more, or whether both processes occurred together. The FTC opened an inquiry into how companion providers test and monitor potential effects on children and teens. An inquiry is a demand for information, and the short study is evidence about specific conditions over four weeks. Neither can tell us what a teenager might experience after spending a year with a product designed to keep them engaged.32

For a companion product, more time spent chatting can look like success on a dashboard. The same behavior could concern a parent or a clinician. A company should be able to describe the circumstances in which it wants a user to spend less time with the product and what it has built to make that happen. If a product cannot accommodate that outcome, I’d take any assurance about its priorities with caution.

Market concentration compounds these questions. The FTC’s study of large cloud and AI developer partnerships describes investment arrangements that can commit developers to buy cloud services from a partner, give partners access to sensitive information, and raise the costs of switching. That evidence identifies mechanisms worth watching. It does not tell us that every AI market will consolidate in the same way. My worry is what happens once a school system, hospital network, or public agency has organized its workflows around a provider it cannot readily leave. A customer in that position may have a difficult time insisting on better audit access, data protections, or a working appeal process.33

I’d judge an automated decision by what happens to the first person it gets wrong. Can they find out what happened? Can they get to someone with the information and authority to fix it? Can the institution identify everybody else affected by the same error? Those capabilities rarely appear in a product demo. They are precisely what a buyer should ask to see.

How a bad outcome becomes a catastrophe#

The most serious argument for catastrophic AI risk starts with a change in what people allow systems to do. A model that returns text can give terrible advice. A system that can act on behalf of a person or an institution can carry out a series of decisions before a reviewer sees the result. Give many such systems responsibility for important processes, and the failures may interact. Give a system sufficiently broad authority and poor oversight, and shutting things down safely could become harder than removing a product from a webpage.

There are several paths to severe harm. People can deliberately misuse capable systems. Institutions can delegate consequential tasks to systems that fail under pressure. Different organizations can adopt similar systems and discover their errors are correlated. A more contested scenario involves future AI systems acting against efforts to constrain them and becoming difficult to bring back under control. Each path rests on different claims. An outage, a successful lab evaluation, and a judgment about future autonomy provide evidence for different parts of the argument.34

I take the loss-of-control argument seriously in part because the authority granted to a system is a commercial decision. An unreliable agent can still be profitable if it saves enough time and somebody else bears the cost of the failure. A rival that promises to do the whole job can put pressure on a company whose product still stops for approval. An institution may expand the system’s remit gradually after months of acceptable performance, without ever testing the fully expanded version against the conditions under which the human fallback would be most needed. There is no requirement that a deployment feel reckless to the people authorizing it, one permission at a time.

There’s a substantial gap between that argument and a confident forecast of extinction. A severe loss-of-control scenario would require some combination of sustained capability, real-world access, failures of oversight, and difficulty interrupting or containing the system in time. The February 2026 International AI Safety Report assessed that systems at the time lacked the capabilities to pose the loss-of-control risks it described, while improving in relevant areas such as autonomous operation. It also reviewed how hard it is to extrapolate from particular evaluations to systems in a changing world. The sensible question to ask of new evidence is which assumption it changes. A result about longer tasks updates one part of the case. An effective intervention under realistic conditions updates another.35

Consider a measurement that frequently gets translated into a claim about how long an AI can work. METR’s 50% task-completion time horizon is the human-expert completion time for tasks in its suite at which a model is estimated to succeed half the time. The clock describes the difficulty of the tasks as measured through human performance. Most tasks in the suite are more clearly specified and easier to score than the work people do inside institutions. A rising time horizon is evidence that performance has improved on longer tasks in the suite. Anyone using that benchmark to decide whether an agent can handle a duty where failure is expensive still needs to test the actual duty at the reliability it requires.36

Cybersecurity and biological misuse deserve similarly precise questions about what changes when assistance becomes widely available. A capability evaluation can show that access to an AI system improves a participant’s performance on a bounded task. A real incident depends on the participant’s starting skill, access, resources, and the safeguards outside the model. The evaluation can justify stronger controls even before we have a reliable incident rate. It cannot, on its own, tell us the prevalence of severe harm in the world. For military uses, the International Committee of the Red Cross has pressed for rules on autonomous weapons and meaningful limits on the circumstances in which force can be delegated. The question there is also one of authority. Who had the information, time, and ability to intervene before an irreversible act?37

I put limited weight on public extinction probabilities. Any single figure compresses judgments about what future models will be able to do, where they’ll be deployed, what other actors will do in response, and whether a failure can be stopped. People can disagree on the stakes because they disagree about just one of those conditions. I’d rather learn which one. I’d also like to know which precautions they would support under both their estimate and mine.

There’s quite a lot to agree on before anyone settles the extinction debate. A company can limit what an agent is authorized to do, make important actions traceable, test whether people can interrupt it, and preserve a way to operate if it has to be taken offline. An independent evaluator can try to reproduce important safety claims under realistic conditions. Organizations can report serious incidents so that the same failure is visible before it spreads. The case for these measures gets stronger as the potential damage rises, the action becomes harder to reverse, and fewer people are in a position to catch the error.

What is worth building?#

In a Swedish randomized mammography trial involving nearly 106,000 women, researchers compared an AI-supported screening workflow with standard double reading by radiologists. The AI helped determine which examinations needed one or two readers and assisted detection. The study reported higher sensitivity, the same measured specificity, and a lower reading workload in the AI-supported group. Its primary outcome, the rate of cancers diagnosed between screening rounds, met the prespecified non-inferiority criterion. The point estimate was lower with AI, with uncertainty wide enough that the result supports non-inferiority rather than a conclusion of superiority on that outcome. This is the kind of evidence I want more of. The researchers tested a real workflow, specified what they would count as an unacceptable outcome, and checked what happened beyond the initial screen.38

AlphaFold offers a different kind of gain. The public AlphaFold database reported predicted structures for more than 214 million protein sequences in its 2024 description. A structural prediction gives a scientist a place to start, makes some lines of inquiry cheaper, and comes with confidence information that helps determine when further validation is needed. This was a different kind of AI from the chatbots that dominate the public debate. It belongs in the essay because the actual capability, the person who can use it, and the thing that still needs checking are all unusually clear.39

Scientific benefits in one domain don’t answer a resident who has to live beside a facility, and a badly sited facility tells us little about the value of a screening program. Once you stop trying to settle both disputes with one judgment about AI, you can ask for terms that make sense locally. A developer seeking guaranteed electricity service can bear more of the risk that its demand never arrives. A school buying a tutor can test what its students retain. An employer automating junior work can show how it will train the next cohort. An agency using a risk score can run a working appeal. A lab giving an agent consequential authority can demonstrate that someone else can interrupt it.

The choices I’d defend most strongly are ones that make it possible to change course. A community with site-level reporting can renegotiate or enforce a permit if actual conditions diverge from the application. A teacher who still gives unaided assessments can see whether a tool is doing the wrong work for the student. An institution with accessible records can investigate a decision after someone contests it. A company with a practiced fallback can turn off an automated system without facing a choice between continuing to use it and shutting down the service altogether. All of those arrangements cost money and effort. That’s a reason to put them into the price of deployment while the buyer still has leverage.

I started with the bottle because it’s a good example of how far a number can travel from the decision it was supposed to inform. It’s possible to spend hours debating drops per prompt and still be unable to say what a data center will draw from the local water system in August. It’s possible to celebrate better homework and miss what happened when the exam started. A chart of the fastest models in the world can tell you remarkably little about what the company buying one intends to let it do.

I’m optimistic about AI because I’ve seen what happens when a cheap enough tool lets someone try something they would have abandoned before they started. The way to protect that possibility is to make the costs of a particular use visible while the people affected still have a say. The homeowner should be able to read the permit and find out what happens in a drought. The student should be able to close the laptop and solve the problem. The person denied a service should know who can reconsider the decision. If the people selling the system can explain its capabilities in extraordinary detail, they can afford to be at least as precise about what happens when it fails.
