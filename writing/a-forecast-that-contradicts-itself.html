A forecast can be close to the outcome and still contradict itself.

Prediction markets offer a tempting evaluation shortcut: wait for the event to resolve, then score the probability each contract carried. That is necessary, but families of related contracts offer a second question that can be answered before the outcome is known. Could all of the probabilities be true at once?

This sounds elementary. It is. That is why it is useful. If a model fails a constraint implied by the event definitions, no amount of sophistication in its architecture can make the joint forecast coherent.

Two geometries#

Imagine three mutually exclusive, exhaustive temperature bins. If a model assigns them probabilities 0.40, 0.35, and 0.32, the sum is 1.07. Each number might look plausible on its own. Together they describe an impossible distribution. A partition must sum to one.

Now imagine contracts for a game total exceeding 20 points and exceeding 30 points. The second event is contained in the first. Its probability cannot be larger. A model that reports 0.48 for the lower threshold and 0.55 for the higher one has reversed the order of the events.

These are different structures. The first is a partition; the second is a cumulative distribution across nested thresholds. Both provide a test that needs no resolved outcome. The test only needs the contract definitions and a snapshot of the forecasts at the same time.

Accuracy is not enough#

A Brier score or an error against the next observed price treats each forecast as a prediction to be judged against a target. It does not, by itself, ask whether neighboring contracts agree. A model can improve an average error metric while producing probabilities that violate the ladder they jointly describe.

In work with Bruce Nshuti Hirwa, we use this coherence constraint as a separate evaluation axis for prediction-market ladders. The point is not to replace outcome-based scoring. It is to measure something outcome-based scoring can miss. We compare matched models and score their raw forecasts. If we first project every forecast onto a coherent set, we remove the very signal we wanted to inspect.

A contradiction is not a trade#

The numbers above are model probabilities. They are not executable market quotes. Even a similar pattern in displayed market prices does not establish an arbitrage. Last trades may have happened at different times. Bids and asks differ. Fees, size limits, collateral, settlement rules, and the ability to execute both legs determine whether an apparent lock can actually be captured.

For the nested-threshold example, the meaningful execution check is whether the higher-threshold contract can be sold at its current bid for more than the lower-threshold contract can be bought at its current ask, after costs, under identical settlement terms. A violation in a historical series of last prices is evidence to inspect, not a realized profit.

An audit before resolution#

I would keep four records for every ladder: the contract definitions, a synchronized snapshot, the raw model probabilities, and a simple baseline such as persistence. For partitions, measure the absolute distance of the probability sum from one. For nested events, measure each reversal of the required order. Then resolve the contracts and score accuracy and calibration separately.

The split between training and testing matters too. Contracts on the same underlying event are related observations, so putting siblings on opposite sides of a split can leak information. Repeated windows from one ladder are not independent experimental units. The uncertainty calculation should respect that grouping.

There is a broader data lesson here. In another study with Bruce, removing consecutive duplicate prices made a persistence baseline wrong at every retained step by construction. The apparent skill came partly from the way the question was prepared. Coherence checks do not solve that problem, but they share the same discipline: inspect what the evaluation procedure guarantees before interpreting what the model achieved.

The useful boundary#

A coherent forecast can still be badly calibrated. An accurate forecast can still hide internal contradictions. A profitable strategy requires more than either property. The practical gain from a coherence audit is narrower and cleaner: it tells us whether the model's probabilities could describe one world, before we ask how well they predicted that world.
