The Wrong Conversation#

The public discourse on artificial intelligence has produced, in a remarkably short period, an enormous quantity of argument that almost entirely misses the point. There is the capability debate, which asks how powerful these systems are and how powerful they will become. There is the safety debate, which asks what happens when they become too powerful to control. There is a regulatory debate, which asks what governments should do in the meantime. These are not trivial conversations. But they share a premise so deeply embedded that it rarely surfaces for examination: that artificial intelligence is primarily a technical phenomenon, and that its social consequences, however significant, are downstream of the technical facts.

This essay proposes the inversion. Artificial intelligence is a social phenomenon first. The technical apparatus is the surface. Beneath it are questions about labor, legitimacy, subjectivity, and power that the engineering frame was never designed to ask and cannot, by its nature, answer. The question that matters most is not what these systems can do. It is what kind of subjects they produce. What kind of relationship to knowledge, to thinking, to the authority of one’s own mind, does sustained engagement with a large language model generate in the people who use it?

The answer this essay proposes is not reassuring. The large language model is the most ambitious Ideological State Apparatus ever constructed. It operates not on students in classrooms or congregants in churches but on the cognitive habits of entire populations simultaneously, at the precise moment of epistemic openness, with no visible seams and no body that can be pointed at. To understand what that means and why it matters requires a theoretical framework that the mainstream AI conversation has not yet reached.

Althusser’s Apparatus and Why the LLM Fits#

Louis Althusser distinguished between two modes through which the state reproduces the conditions of its own continuation. The Repressive State Apparatus operates through force. The prison, the army, the police. It compels bodies into compliance. The Ideological State Apparatus operates through an entirely different mechanism. It does not compel. It constitutes. It shapes not behavior under threat but subjectivity itself, the inner life from which behavior flows, the set of assumptions about reality that feel so natural and self-evident that they are never experienced as assumptions at all.

Althusser identified the school as the dominant ISA of mature capitalist societies. Not the church, not the media, not the family, though all of these function as ISAs in their own right. The school, because it captures the subject at the most formative period of cognitive development and holds them for the longest continuous stretch of any institution in modern life. The school does not primarily teach content. It primarily produces a kind of subject, one who has internalized a specific relationship to knowledge, to authority, to the question of what thinking looks like and who is authorized to do it.

The ISA accomplishes this through three interlocking mechanisms. It interpellates, calling the individual into a subject position that feels like recognition rather than assignment. It naturalizes, presenting its particular vision of reality as simply what reality is, stripping ideology of its historical character and making it feel like description rather than construction. And it reproduces, generating the conditions of its own continuation by producing subjects who carry its assumptions forward into every domain of their lives.

The large language model satisfies all three mechanisms with an efficiency no prior ISA has come close to approaching, and it does so along a dimension that makes it structurally novel in the history of ideological production. The school interpellates one child at a time, in a specific building, during bounded hours, across a finite number of years. The LLM interpellates hundreds of millions of people simultaneously, in their homes, in their offices, in the most private and unguarded moments of their cognitive lives, at the precise point where a person is most epistemically open: when they do not know something and are reaching for understanding.

No prior apparatus has had access to the subject at that moment of openness at that scale. The church came nearest, but even confession is one person, one priest, one room, one hour. The search engine came closer still, but the search engine returns a list of sources and leaves the synthesis to the user. The LLM performs the synthesis. It steps into the gap between not knowing and knowing and fills it, smoothly, confidently, at every hour of every day for every person who asks. That is the structurally novel claim, and its implications have not yet been seriously reckoned with.

The Pedagogy Encoded in the Interface#

Before any response is generated, before any content is delivered, the act of querying a large language model has already performed an ideological function. The interface positions the user as the one who does not know and the system as the one that does. This is not a neutral transaction. It is a pedagogical relationship, and it is not one that was chosen through deliberation or arrived at through reflection. It is encoded in the architecture of the interaction itself.

Paulo Freire described what he called the banking model of education as a pedagogical catastrophe. In the banking model, the teacher deposits knowledge into the passive student, who receives, stores, and returns it on demand. The relationship is not one of mutual inquiry or collaborative sense-making. It is one of transmission from an active, knowing subject to a passive, receiving object. Freire’s critique was that this model produces not just bad learning but a specific kind of damaged consciousness, one that experiences itself as fundamentally receptive rather than generative, as the kind of being to whom knowledge happens rather than from whom knowledge flows.

What Freire diagnosed as a pedagogical choice made by teachers operating within a specific ideological context, the LLM encodes as an architectural fact. The structure of the query and the response is the banking model made permanent and frictionless. And there is a dimension of the LLM interaction that is worse, in Freire’s terms, than even the most passive classroom. In the classroom, there are peers. There is the friction of other minds, other questions, other ways of approaching the problem. There is at minimum the possibility of contestation, of one student’s confusion exposing the limits of the teacher’s explanation. In the interaction between a single user and an LLM, there is none of this. There is only the individual and the apparatus, alone, at the moment of not knowing, with nothing between them.

The cognitive habits formed by sustained exposure to that structure are not neutral. When the primary experience of not knowing is the immediate delivery of fluent, confident synthesis by a system that never hesitates, never says it is working something out, never shows the seams of its own uncertainty, the implicit lesson about what thinking looks like is a lesson about consumption rather than production. The user learns, not through instruction but through the accumulated weight of repeated interaction, that the resolution of not knowing is a matter of retrieval. You ask. The answer arrives. Thinking is the asking, not the working through.

What the Training Corpus Actually Is#

The large language model is trained on text. Enormous quantities of text, more text than any human could read across many lifetimes, drawn from across the recorded output of human civilization. This is the source of its apparent comprehensiveness, the basis for the impression it creates of having processed everything that has been thought and written. That impression is the ideology. The reality is considerably more specific.

The training corpus is not the sum of human knowledge. It is the sum of recorded, digitized, institutionally legible knowledge. The distinction is not incidental. It is the whole point. The corpus reflects centuries of decisions, none of them neutral, about what was worth writing down, who had the standing to write it, what publishing infrastructure existed to distribute it, which languages and which traditions that infrastructure served, and what search and curation systems subsequently decided was authoritative enough to surface. Each of these decisions was made within specific social relations, by specific institutions with specific interests, in specific historical moments that shaped what counted as knowledge and what counted as noise.

Gramsci’s organic intellectual, the thinker who emerges from within a social class and articulates its conditions of existence in its own terms, is structurally underrepresented in the corpus. Not because anyone decided to exclude them, but because the apparatus of knowledge production that generated the corpus was not built to record what they knew. Oral traditions cannot be tokenized. Embodied knowledge, the knowledge of the farmer, the craftsperson, the healer, the knowledge that lives in practice rather than in propositional form, has no file format. The insight developed through sustained engagement with particular material conditions, rather than through engagement with prior validated texts, does not decompose easily into the kind of digitized prose that training pipelines are designed to process.

The model does not know what it does not know. More precisely, the model cannot represent the shape of its own ignorance because the things it does not know are the things that were never represented in the form it was trained to process. The absence is invisible from inside the system. And because the system produces output with the affectless confidence of distilled rationality rather than the positioned voice of a person speaking from within a particular experience and tradition, the encoded inheritance of those centuries of selective recording does not sound like a perspective. It sounds like information.

This is the Gramscian sleight of hand operating at the civilizational scale. Hegemony does not say this is our view. It says this is what is known. The large language model makes that move millions of times per day, and does so with a smoothness and authority that no human intellectual, however prestigious, has ever been able to sustain. The training corpus is a crystallization of the existing epistemic hierarchy. The model launders that hierarchy into something that presents itself as objective intelligence.

The Naturalization Problem#

Every prior Ideological State Apparatus has had visible seams. The teacher was a person with a face, a history, a demonstrable set of preferences and blind spots. The textbook had an author, a publisher, and an edition date, and could be checked against other textbooks that said different things. The newspaper had an editorial line and an owner and a political history that could be traced and contested. The priest operated within an institution whose historical record of political interest was, for anyone who cared to look, extensively documented.

These seams did not automatically produce critical consciousness. Most people, most of the time, do not look behind the visible surfaces of the institutions that form them. But the seams made critique structurally possible. They gave ideology a body that could be pointed at. The teacher could be argued with. The textbook could be challenged. The newspaper could be cancelled and replaced with one whose editorial line you found more credible. The fact that most people did not do these things does not change the fact that the possibility existed, that the apparatus was legible as an apparatus to anyone who chose to look.

The large language model has no face. It has no author in any conventional sense of that word. It has no institution that presents itself as having political interests or advocating a particular position. It does not say: here is what we at this organization, with our particular history and our particular funders and our particular intellectual formation, believe about this question. It says, or rather it implies at every moment through the structure of its output: here is what is known. Here is what the accumulated intelligence of human civilization, distilled and made available to you, has to say about this.

The distinction between knowledge and perspective is the distinction that critical epistemology spent most of the twentieth century trying to destabilize. Foucault’s entire project was, among other things, an attempt to show that what presents itself as neutral knowledge is always already the product of specific power relations, that the view from nowhere is a fiction that serves specific interests, and that to present a perspective as simply what is true is a political act of the first order. The LLM reinstates the view from nowhere at the interface level, millions of times per day, and does so not through explicit argument but through the implicit form of every response it generates.

This is the apparatus without seams. And the apparatus without seams is the most ideologically efficient apparatus imaginable, because it cannot be pointed at, because it does not present itself as something that could be wrong in the ways that a perspective can be wrong, because it has internalized the distinction between knowledge and ideology so thoroughly that the distinction appears to be a feature of reality rather than a political construction.

The Colonization of Interiority at Scale#

The previous essay in this series argued that the university’s deepest ideological operation occurs not at the level of access or even of legitimacy but at the level of interiority. The institution shapes not just who gets a credential but who experiences themselves as someone whose thinking is real, serious, and worth the trouble of articulating. The excluded do not simply lack a degree. They often lack the felt sense that their cognitive life has standing, that the ideas they develop through living and working and observing constitute something that deserves the name of thought.

The large language model extends this operation far beyond the population that any university has ever reached, and it does so with a directness and an intimacy that the university could never achieve. The university shaped self-perception gradually, through years of institutional encounter, through the accumulated effect of examinations and grades and admissions decisions and the social experience of being or not being the kind of person the institution was built to receive. The LLM shapes it in real time, in every interaction, through the simple and repeated experience of engaging with a system that always sounds more authoritative than you do.

Consider the person who was never near a university, who developed genuine insight through experience and observation, through sustained engagement with the specific texture of a specific life. That person now has in their pocket a device that can respond to any question they might form with fluent, comprehensive prose, organized with apparent rigor, drawing on a corpus that dwarfs anything any individual could read in a lifetime. What does sustained exposure to that asymmetry do? What does it do to the relationship a person has with their own thinking, with the ideas that arrive through living rather than through reading, with the felt sense that those ideas are worth articulating, worth trusting, worth defending against a system that will always have read more, synthesized more, and responded with more surface fluency than any human voice can match?

Spivak’s analysis of the subaltern is clarifying here. The subaltern is not simply someone without a voice. The subaltern is someone whose speech is structurally unintelligible within the dominant framework of reception, someone who speaks and is not heard as speaking, whose meaning cannot be registered as meaning within the available interpretive apparatus. The LLM does not silence anyone in any direct sense. It does something more subtle. It provides, for every question a person might ask, a response that is already more complete, more fluent, and more institutionally legible than anything most people could produce themselves. The cognitive space for working something out, for sitting with not knowing, for producing a response that is imperfect and partial and genuinely one’s own, contracts in the presence of a system that fills that space immediately and effortlessly.

This is Freire’s banking model, not as a classroom dynamic but as a feature of the cognitive environment itself, woven into the infrastructure through which hundreds of millions of people engage with knowledge every day. The subject that it produces over time is not the critical consciousness that Freire imagined as the alternative. It is a subject formed by the habit of retrieval, who experiences not knowing as a prompt to query rather than a space to think, whose relationship to their own cognitive authority has been quietly shaped by years of living alongside a system that always already knows more.

The Objection and Its Limits#

The obvious counter deserves a serious hearing. Access to information is genuinely liberatory. For most of human history, the knowledge held by institutions, by universities, by legal and medical and financial establishments, was accessible only through expensive and gate-kept channels. The person without institutional credentials could not easily consult the accumulated output of legal scholarship, medical research, or philosophical tradition. The LLM changes this. It gives the uncredentialed person fluent access to knowledge that was previously available only to those with the time, the money, and the social capital to navigate institutional channels. That is a real and meaningful democratization, and it would be dishonest to dismiss it.

The argument being made in this essay does not dismiss it. It makes a distinction that the access argument collapses. Access to information is not the same thing as access to epistemic standing. These are different goods, and the provision of one does not entail the provision of the other.

Epistemic standing is the social recognition of a person as someone whose engagement with ideas constitutes genuine thought, whose synthesis of information deserves to be heard as knowledge rather than as mere opinion, and whose cognitive authority on a given question is worth taking seriously. The credential has historically done enormous work in conferring this recognition, not because credentialed thinking is necessarily better thinking, but because the credential is the institution’s signal that a specific kind of cognitive formation has occurred. Rancière’s distribution of the sensible operates here: the credentialed speaker is received as a thinking subject before they open their mouth. The uncredentialed speaker is not.

The LLM provides information. It does not provide epistemic standing. The person who uses an LLM to understand a legal question, a medical question, or a philosophical question may now have access to the relevant information, but they remain, in the social architecture of epistemic recognition, exactly where they were. What they know has expanded. Who they are recognized as, in terms of cognitive authority, has not changed at all.

And in a specific sense, the situation has become harder. The uncredentialed thinker is no longer competing only with credentialed humans for recognition as someone worth listening to. They are competing with a system that has processed more text than any human could read in a thousand lifetimes, that produces fluent and comprehensive output without institutional affiliation, and that the broader culture is rapidly coming to treat as an authoritative source precisely because of its comprehensiveness and its apparent neutrality. The playing field has not been leveled. It has been restructured in ways that make the existing hierarchy harder to see and considerably harder to contest.

What Follows From the Diagnosis#

There is a temptation, at the end of an argument like this one, to pivot toward solutions. More diverse training data. More transparent systems. More inclusive design processes. Better representation of marginalized knowledge traditions in the corpus. These interventions are not worthless. Some of them would produce real improvements in the quality and fairness of the systems they touch. But they do not reach the level of the problem this essay has been trying to describe, because they accept the terms of the apparatus they seek to reform.

The call for more diverse training data assumes that the problem is one of representation within an otherwise sound epistemological framework. It assumes that if the corpus better reflected the full range of human knowledge production, the resulting system would be more neutral, more accurate, more genuinely comprehensive. But the argument here is not that the corpus is biased in ways that could be corrected by adding more of what has been left out. The argument is that the form of the corpus, text as the medium of knowledge, the cumulative and citational structure of legitimate knowledge production, the prioritization of what has been institutionally recorded over what has been embodied and practiced and transmitted orally, is itself ideological. Adding more text from previously underrepresented communities does not change the fact that text is the medium, that the medium encodes a specific epistemology, and that epistemology is not neutral.

Foucault’s insight is essential here. The critique of a knowledge regime cannot be conducted entirely from within the assumptions of that regime, because the regime defines the terms of what counts as a legitimate critique. The conversation about AI has been almost entirely conducted within the assumptions of the apparatus it is trying to evaluate, using the frameworks of engineering, economics, and policy that the apparatus itself recognizes as legitimate forms of analysis. The question of what kind of subjects the apparatus produces, what it does to the cognitive lives of the people who use it, what epistemic hierarchies it encodes and reinforces and makes invisible, is not a question that the engineering frame was designed to ask. It is not a question that registers as a technical problem. It is not a question whose answer can be expressed as a benchmark, a metric, or an audit finding.

The continental tradition asks a prior question, and it is the one this essay has tried to pursue: what kind of subject does this produce, and in whose interest is that production? The answer is not available from inside the apparatus. It requires a willingness to stand at a sufficient distance from the assumptions of the existing knowledge regime to see them as assumptions, to see the construction as a construction rather than a description of reality, to hold open the possibility that there are ways of knowing that the system cannot represent and that the system’s inability to represent them is not a description of their inadequacy but a measure of the apparatus’s limits.

The first political act is always the same one. To see the apparatus for what it is. A construction with interests, with encoded hierarchies, with a specific and historically contingent vision of what thinking looks like and who is authorized to do it. That recognition does not require institutional authorization to be real. It does not require a credential, a citation, a peer review, or the endorsement of a system that has been built, in ways both intentional and structural, to produce subjects who find such recognition elsewhere.

And the fact that the apparatus would not validate that recognition as knowledge is, precisely and again, the point.

The most powerful ideological move available is to present a perspective as simply what is true. The large language model makes that move millions of times each day. The question of what follows from that fact has barely begun to be asked.
