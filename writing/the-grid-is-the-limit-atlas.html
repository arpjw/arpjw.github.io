A first public-source edition accompanying The Grid Is the Limit. Evidence cutoff: November 17, 2026. All quantitative records are in atlas-data.csv, with dates, definitions and source IDs. The operator extraction record identifies the NYISO table rows and ISO-NE workbook cells. The validator checks provenance and generates the standardized 1 GW campus cases. This is a decision map, not a ranking of states or a forecast of customer outages.

The question the atlas can answer

If a proposed AI campus asks for 1 GW of electric service, where is the binding constraint likely to be, what evidence would establish it, and how much of the proposed load is already reflected in public forecasts? The answer may be a generation adequacy requirement, a particular transmission interface, a distribution substation, a transformer lead time, a customer's collateral, a local permit, or a combination. A regional annual peak is a useful first coordinate. It is not a site-specific connection study.

The atlas uses a common set of seven operator footprints: PJM, ERCOT, California ISO, MISO, SPP East, NYISO, and ISO New England. These are not the entire United States and they are not interchangeable geographies. SPP's 2026 resource report is for its East balancing authority. CAISO does not equal the state of California. MISO spans part of Canada. New York City can be constrained even if the New York Control Area has adequate aggregate resources. A 1 GW facility also represents very different shares of local load depending on its node. These distinctions prevent a national “best region” table from posing as engineering.

The three decision horizons are 2027, 2030, and 2035. At 2027, signed agreements, equipment orders and completed studies matter more than a speculative 2035 technology mix. At 2030, construction schedules, resource accreditation and load realization dominate. At 2035, fuel, climate, electrification, retirement and transmission scenarios widen. The same number of MW should carry less confidence the further it is from operation.

PJM: a capacity signal and an uncertain request pipeline

PJM is the clearest case of a measured procurement gap. Its 2028/29 capacity auction secured 138.3178 GW through the auction and included 10.8638 GW under Fixed Resource Requirement arrangements, 6.8314 GW below a 156.013 GW reliability requirement. This does not mean 6.8314 GW of firm load will be shed. It means the auction did not procure the modeled target under its assumptions. The exact risk depends on weather, outages, imports, new capacity and demand realization. The mathematics companion explains why LOLE and EUE are better risk outputs than a single subtraction.

The load side is moving. PJM's January forecast put 2030 summer peak at 183.008 GW and 2035 at 216.872 GW. It revised near-term estimates down from the previous vintage after scrutinizing large-load adjustments, EV adoption and economic inputs. A November FERC filing reported 61.322 GW of 2030 adjustment requests as of September, compared with 33.707 GW incorporated in the 2026 forecast. The additional requests were not yet vetted. They must not be added to 183.008 GW or treated as a new operating fleet. For a 2027 campus, the first question is whether its proposed capacity is already in the relevant zonal adjustment and whether its local transmission study and service obligation are complete. At 2030, compare realized project cohorts with the request and forecast vintages. At 2035, test multiple demand paths and retirement and interconnection schedules.

Connection gate: identify the exact PJM zone and substation, the customer service obligation and construction milestone, the approved import capability under contingencies, the associated generation and capacity procurement plan, and whether non-firm service is legally and operationally available. An RTO-wide auction result cannot answer the substation question.

ERCOT: the largest visible gap between inquiry and operation

ERCOT's March 2026 status briefing showed 410.618 GW in requests seeking connection by 2030. About 293.7 GW had no studies submitted; 86.6 GW was under review; 21.3 GW had an approved planning study; 3.2 GW was approved to energize but not operating; and 5.8 GW was shown as observed energized. The figures are stage counts in a changing request inventory. They do not constitute a load forecast. The graphic's observed-energized category is also not the total existing ERCOT system load. A February briefing used a different snapshot and total, so their difference is not a month of measured growth.

ERCOT's April 2026 preliminary long-term forecast projected a 367.790 GW 2032 peak in a request-inclusive method. ERCOT itself said this was likely higher than realized growth and proposed realization adjustments in May. The December 2025 CDR said its base reserve margin would turn negative in 2028 under its adjusted load forecast and prescribed resources, while also showing alternative large-load curtailment cases. That is a conditional planning warning, not a prediction that the grid will lose power in 2028. A 2027 campus needs to move from study and security to a specific energized first phase. A 2030 campus needs cohort-specific realization and credible supply and network dates. A 2035 comparison cannot simply extend ERCOT's preliminary series, which stops in 2032.

Connection gate: review the large-load interconnection study, load-ramp and ride-through requirements, substation and transformer delivery, local transmission contingency, expected 2027 or 2030 phase sizes, and tested curtailment rights. A request at a favorable node may proceed while another request of equal size waits. The atlas does not infer which.

California ISO: a near-term surplus with a different long-term question

CAISO's 2026 Summer Loads and Resources Assessment found a modeled 2.547 GW surplus above its one-in-ten-year supply-shortage criterion for its RA-eligible portfolio. It ran 500 full-year hourly scenarios, and its measure concerns possible emergency actions, not actual household outages. It also projected a 52.940 GW 50/50 summer peak by 2030. These are not contradictory: a 2026 modeled surplus can coexist with a larger 2030 demand forecast and with local bottlenecks. CAISO cautions that rare, coincident heat, wildfire and drought conditions remain outside the assurance of a single adequacy result.

California's substantial battery deployment improves evening flexibility, but a 1 GW new campus would require proof of deliverability and a charging plan through its particular risk hours. At 2027, identify whether the site sits behind a transmission or distribution constraint. At 2030, compare actual resource commissioning and import contracts with the load increase. At 2035, evaluate multi-day weather and neighboring-region demand, since the same heat dome can reduce the assumption that imports will be available. The atlas does not transform the 2026 2.547 GW adequacy surplus into “space for two campuses.”

Connection gate: obtain utility and CAISO studies for the site, include local deliverability and distribution capacity, and test the campus under both summer evening and multi-day stress. A 50/50 system peak is not an import-right certificate.

MISO: member plans and seasonal accreditation

The 2026 OMS-MISO survey reported five-year member load-growth forecasts and requests of 3.1% to 5.1% annually. Utilities planned an average 15 GW of new accredited summer capacity each year over the survey horizon, including about 4 GW per year expected to use an expedited study pathway. These are plans. Their reliability value depends on completion, accreditation, season and location. The 2026 Planning Resource Auction cleared a summer margin of 11.4% versus an initial target of 7.9%, and showed more summer surplus than the prior year. The auction result is near-term evidence against a simple imminent-collapse story; it is not proof that every future large-load request is covered.

MISO's risk migrates across seasons as load and resources change. A summer-only accounting can miss winter fuel and availability stress. For 2027, check the local resource zone and whether the campus is already reflected in member load submissions. For 2030, the key question is which planned additions have become operating, accredited, deliverable capacity. For 2035, a survey release ending in 2031/32 does not supply a defensible point forecast. The atlas therefore leaves that cell empty.

Connection gate: secure zonal load forecasts, the affected local resource zone's import limits, seasonal accredited capacity, the relevant utility's service tariff and network-upgrade schedule. A footprint-wide CAGR does not provide these.

SPP East: two futures in one report

SPP's 2026 summer adequacy report reported a 17.2% accredited-capacity reserve margin for the East balancing authority and about 5.807 GW above its total resource adequacy requirement. Under existing resources only, the East BAA becomes deficient against its requirement beginning in 2029 and reaches an 11.268 GW deficit by 2031. Under the same report's anticipated new-resource case, the margin reaches 22.7% by 2031. These are not a contradictory pair of forecasts. They expose the construction and interconnection execution variable. The report itself cautions that proposed resources may miss their dates.

SPP also distinguishes its old base reserve margin from the newer accredited-capacity margin. A 17.2% ACAP result should not be compared to a 17% base PRM as if they used the same numerator. The report ends in 2031, so this atlas does not project a 2035 SPP result. For a 2027 campus, the question is a local load-responsible entity's accredited capacity and the relevant line, not the full 5.807 GW aggregate excess. For 2030, track whether anticipated additions actually commission and whether the added campus was in the LRE's forecast.

Connection gate: determine the East BAA's applicable LRE, its accredited resource and purchase commitments, the project's status in local load forecasts, and the affected transmission contingency. Separate the existing-only and anticipated-resource cases in every presentation.

New York and New England: local delivery and a changing season

The 2026 NYISO Gold Book, Table I-1a, puts the New York Control Area's expected-weather baseline summer peak at 31.880 GW in 2027, 32.250 GW in 2030 and 33.140 GW in 2035. Its 2035/36 winter baseline is 28.480 GW. The table includes modeled large loads and transmission and distribution losses. NYISO's separate June reliability presentation gives 32.0244 GW for the 2027/28 IRM preliminary base case after adding behind-the-meter net-generation forecast values to Gold Book values. That is a different planning case from the 31.880 GW calendar-year Gold Book baseline. NYISO's reliability planning draft identifies changing transmission flows and voltage needs, including from large loads. New York City and Long Island have local delivery constraints that an aggregate NYCA peak cannot dissolve. A 1 GW site in one zone cannot borrow the statewide balance without a deliverability study.

The published ISO New England 2026 CELT workbook, Table 1.5.1, gives 25.290, 25.738 and 26.849 GW for net 50/50 summer peaks in 2027, 2030 and 2035. Its normal-weather winter 2035/36 peak is 26.411 GW, close to that summer figure but still below it in these particular annual forecasts. The operator's outlook projects 9% growth in annual net energy between 2026 and 2035 and a return to winter peaking sometime in the 2030s. That broader outlook does not mean the 2035/36 normal-weather winter value already exceeds the 2035 50/50 summer value. Seasonal weather definitions and a later crossover year matter. The timing of winter peaking matters for a campus with constant demand and for fuel adequacy. At 2027, the actual CELT zone and utility study decide. At 2030 and 2035, winter load, fuel deliverability, imports and project realization must be jointly tested rather than extending a summer peak trend.

Connection gate: for NYISO, a zone-specific transmission and voltage study checked against the Gold Book baseline and current reliability assessment; for ISO-NE, a node-level service study checked against both CELT seasonal peaks and winter resource and fuel assessments. The shared lesson is that “Northeast” is not a planning boundary.

Where constraints show up in prices

The forecasts above say how much load operators expect; locational prices show where the network already separates supply from demand. Figure A1 puts seven markets side by side for November 2025 through October 2026, using each operator's published price components wherever they exist. New York, New England, MISO and CAISO use the same measure, a simple hourly mean of the congestion component. PJM comes from its Independent Market Monitor's load-weighted zonal tables for January through September 2026, because PJM's own data interface requires a key. ERCOT publishes no congestion component, so its zones are measured against ERCOT's bus-average hub; ERCOT prices carry no marginal-loss term, so those spreads are congestion differences. SPP's data portal requires a session token, so its panel uses the regional values stated by SPP's Market Monitor for 2025.

The pattern is consistent with the regional profiles above. Dominion's day-ahead congestion component averaged $+25.75/MWh in the first nine months of 2026. MISO's southern hubs averaged -10.10 (Arkansas) to -8.40 (Louisiana) $/MWh, on the export side of MISO's internal south-to-north transfer limit. ERCOT's Panhandle hub sat -7.04 $/MWh below the bus average, and CAISO's SDG&E load point +2.00 above its energy reference. None of these averages says whether a particular site can connect; that depends on local interconnection studies.

The standardized 1 GW experiment

The generated campus file contains 42 rows: seven regions, three years and two service cases. The firm case assumes 1 GW import at the system peak and 8.76 TWh/year at constant use. The verified-flexible case assumes import capped at 0.7 GW for four daily hours that include the risk hours, and 1 GW otherwise, for 8.322 TWh/year. Every row says regional feasibility: unassessed. The file is deliberately repetitive: its purpose is to keep the customer assumption fixed while a future engineer supplies site-specific constraints. It does not allocate 1 GW to each region simultaneously, compute a utility bill, or show that any operator would accredit the flexibility as assumed.

The experiment should be evaluated in five gates. Power: what is the campus's maximum import, ramp, power factor, fault behavior, onsite generation and backup obligation? Network: what is the delivery limit under normal operation and the relevant contingencies, and what upgrade solves the binding constraint? Resources: what accredited and fuel-secure supply will cover the campus at risk hours, and who carries the procurement obligation? Time: which contracts, studies, permits, equipment orders and construction milestones are firm enough for 2027, 2030 or 2035 service? Cost: who pays for a dedicated upgrade, a shared network upgrade, capacity procurement and a stranded asset if the campus shrinks or cancels? A sixth gate is verification: how will the customer demonstrate curtailment and actual energized load?

No public RTO-wide table answers all six. This is the atlas's main finding. The publishable national story is the divergence between requests, credible projects and delivered power, plus the need to price that uncertainty. A claim that one region can host another 1 GW should wait for a named node and a study. A claim that a region will fail because of a 1 GW request should meet the same standard.

Data dictionary and update rules

region names the operator footprint, not a state. metric preserves the operator's quantity and scenario. target_year is the year being forecast or assessed; source_date is the document vintage. value is blank when not extracted or verified. unit may be MW, percent or percent/year and must never be silently converted across metrics. evidence_class distinguishes forecast, preliminary forecast, request, auction, assessment, scenario, observed status and missing. scope and caveat record boundaries. source_id joins to the source log. A later update should add a new vintage or a documented correction, not overwrite an old forecast in place. The atlas validator enforces populated provenance and explicit missingness.

This edition does not calculate regional LOLE, EUE, hosting capacity, connection time, transformer inventory, household rate impact, or a geopolitical score. Those outputs need utility and operator data beyond the public rows collected here. The remaining data checks for any later edition are tracked in the author's research notes.
